# Legal and truth

What an ad made with generated footage may claim and show, what it must say, and what gets one pulled. Read before release; the rules are as of October 2026 and the law moves.

## Truth

- **A demonstration must be real.** Under United States advertising law a product demonstration must accurately depict how the product performs, and a disclaimer does not cure a misleading demonstration (FTC practice). In practice: show only tasks the product really completes, with real screens from real runs, in the time it really takes; never imply speed or scope it does not have. A launch film that contained two wrong answers from the device was re-cut and the errors became the story (2023).
- **Keep a saved real run behind every result on screen.** Before release, every line the product says or writes in the film is reproduced on the product and the recording kept.
- **What the film shows that the product cannot do yet goes on the product backlog as top priority the same day,** so it is true when the film is published. The client decides whether the film waits or the product catches up; the film is never published ahead of the truth.
- **The truest hook in the field was punished for not being true:** an assistant that tells you the name of the person approaching was advertised in 2024, the feature did not exist, the ad was pulled in 2025 and a class action followed.

## Generated people

- **Fake testimonials are banned** (United States, since 2024-10-21). Generated people must read as dramatized characters, never as real customers. A story told by a generated couple to camera is a dramatization; the end card says the people are generated and that what the product does is real, in plain words, every time.
- **New York requires a conspicuous disclosure** when an ad in any medium reaching a New York audience includes a synthetic performer, a generated human not recognizable as an identifiable natural performer (General Business Law 396-b, in effect 2026-06-09; $1,000 first violation, $5,000 after; audio-only ads exempt). A plain line on the end card satisfies the spirit; check the current rule for placement.
- **EU AI Act Article 50** (applied from 2026-08-02): generated or manipulated content that resembles real people, places or events is labelled as such.
- **No real person's face or voice.** Check each generated face for a resemblance to a real person. Never clone a voice without the person's consent. Never build on someone else's video (a 2026 ad that reworked a creator's video drew backlash).
- **Platform labels:** YouTube requires disclosure when AI "generates a realistic scene that didn't actually occur" (this kind of film qualifies); Meta applies "AI info" itself from file signals and requires self-disclosure for political and social-issue ads; Google Ads has an AI label setting with labels required in the EU, India and New York for certain assets (from July 2026); TikTok expects the label on realistic AI content in ads; LinkedIn reads C2PA credentials and badges automatically; X has a voluntary label. Watermarks such as SynthID are in the pixels and survive re-encoding: assume the film is identifiable as AI-made and disclose on your own terms.

## Naming things

- Naming a real company sharpens a line and adds a risk when the film also plays that company's employee; the default is a generic or invented name, checked against real businesses in the launch area.
- Real logos only from their owner's file, never drawn from memory. Never a generated likeness shown as a real photo. Never an invented result.
- Rivals are never named in the film; a named comparison, with sources, belongs in a post that can be edited.

## What a product says on a call

Whether a product that places phone calls must announce that it is an AI is a legal and product question, not the film's: the FCC treats an AI voice as an "artificial voice" under the telephone consumer protection law, and several states have disclosure rules for certain calls. The film shows what the product does; the client and counsel decide what the product does. The product's standing rule in one case: it gives its name and on whose behalf it calls, and it never claims to be human.

## Reception

Backlash bites where AI replaces craft the audience expected from people. In one survey 41 percent of consumers are bothered by AI ads; ads using AI seamlessly hit top-tier cut-through, while visibly AI work did worse; labelling an ad as AI-generated lowers ratings, less so for innovative and technology products. What gets accepted: entertainment first, humour and self-awareness, invisible craft, a product where AI fits, plain disclosure. A film about an AI product is about the best-fitting case there is.

## Music and voices

Use music licensed for ads (ElevenLabs Music on self-serve plans covers commercial use including ads, excluding film, TV and studio games; check the current terms); avoid generators without an official API or with pending label suits. Library voices come with the platform's commercial licence; read it for the voice's use case.

## The release checklist

1. Every result on screen reproduced on the real product and saved.
2. Every voice approved by the client; no real person's voice.
3. Every generated face checked for a resemblance.
4. The end card: the people are generated, what the product does is real.
5. The platform's AI label set where it is a setting.
6. Invented names checked against real ones.
7. No rival named; no real logo not from its owner.
8. The generation log and receipts kept.
