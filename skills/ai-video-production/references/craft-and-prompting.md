# Craft and prompting for AI-generated films and ads

A dense working reference: how to find the idea, open the film, write the lines, build the drama, prompt each video and image model, and fit the result to the platform. Every section is rules, numbers, prompt shapes, checklists and failure modes. Read the section you need; run the checklists before anything ships.

Order of work: idea (1) before hook (2) before lines (3) before shot design (4) before prompts (5, 6) before platform cut-downs (7). Skipping a step does not fail loudly; it produces a film that is busy and empty.

---

## 1. Finding the idea

### 1.1 Insight before ideas

An idea without an insight is decoration. Creativity = novelty + usefulness: ultra-novel but useless is not creative, generic and on-brief is not creative either. Work the intersection of the unexpected and the strategically precise.

**Four points, in order.** Problem -> Insight -> Advantage -> Strategy.
- Problem: the real human or business problem, not the symptom. "Sales are falling" is a symptom; "people do not understand why they need this" is a problem.
- Insight: a hidden truth that flips the view of the problem. Not a fact, not an observation; a truth everyone feels and nobody has said aloud.
- Advantage: the one thing the brand uniquely offers in the light of that insight. One strength, not a feature list.
- Strategy: one directive sentence that tells the creative team what to do, not how.
Each step validates the one before it. If the strategy does not rest on the advantage, the advantage is weak.

**Jobs to be done.** People hire a product, and hire an ad, for a job on three layers: functional (what it does), social (how it changes how others see me), emotional (what I feel while using it). The real competitor is everything that does the same job, not the other brand in the category (the morning milkshake competes with podcasts and bagels). Exercise: write five jobs the product is hired for, three non-obvious competitors per job; the most interesting job with the most unexpected competitor is the direction for the insight.

**Tension spotting.** A tension is the gap between how things should be and how they are. Three kinds:
- Cultural: what society declares vs what people do ("everyone talks about conscious consumption and orders fast fashion at 2 a.m.").
- Category: what the category promises vs delivers ("banks promise partnership, treat you like a queue number").
- Human: what a person wants vs what stands in the way ("wants to eat healthy, hates diet food").
Method: list 5 conventions of the category (what every brand says and shows); for each ask "but what actually happens?"; the gap is the tension. A good tension is uncomfortable and recognizable at once: the listener winces and nods. No discomfort means too mild; no recognition means fabricated.

**How might we.** Write three questions at three altitudes: broad (culture or category), medium (brand and audience, usually the most productive), narrow (product or format). Good: "How might we make choosing insurance feel like writing a letter to someone you love?" Bad: "How might we increase sales?" (a business objective, not a creative question).

**Abstraction laddering.** Go up two steps with "why?", come down one with "how exactly?". Brief says "tell them about the new feature" -> why? "to save time" -> why? "to spend it on what matters" -> down: "show what the person does with the freed hour." The optimal rung is one level above the obvious: too high is generic ("people need happiness"), too low is a tactic ("15% off").

**Assumption mapping.** List at least 10 assumptions about audience, product, competitors, task. Plot each on importance x confidence. The high-importance, low-confidence quadrant is where the strongest insights hide ("the buyer is a man 25-35" -> what if it is his wife?; "they choose between us and competitor X" -> what if they choose between us and doing nothing?).

**Insight quality test, six checks.** True (people recognize themselves)? Fresh (not "people like convenience")? Contains tension? Triggers "yes, exactly, but I never put it that way"? Buildable (leads to ideas)? Specific to this audience and category? Fails two or more: dig again.

**Insight format, one sentence.** "[Audience] wants [X], but [Y stands in the way], because [Z]." Without the "because" it is an observation.

### 1.2 Pick the idea level before generating

Seven levels, from the longest-lived down: business idea (what the company does), brand idea (what it stands for, years), tagline (the brand idea in 5 words or fewer), advertising idea (the central thought across all comms for 3-5 years, recognizable without the logo), campaign idea (3-12 months), non-advertising idea (something useful that lives without ads; test: "remove the campaign, does it still mean something?"), execution (a format, a channel, a mechanic). The number one cause of friction is a level mismatch: a brand manifesto delivered for a shelf talker, an AR filter delivered for a rebrand. "We partner with a famous creator" is an execution, not an idea.

### 1.3 Ideation methods

Prime against the canon first: look at 5 to 7 existing campaigns in the same industry, budget band or emotional register, so you generate toward the gap and not the pattern. Remixing across categories is legitimate (a beverage mechanic moved into beauty); cite the lineage.

Pick three methods from three different families. Default triplet: a structural method, an association method, a perturbation method.

**Structural**
- SIT templates (six patterns behind most award-winning ads; everything is drawn from the product's own "closed world"): Subtraction (remove a key element and show the consequence; the tiny car in white space), Multiplication (copy an element with a twist), Division (separate function from form), Task unification (the packaging becomes the media), Attribute dependency ("the more X, the more Y" where the link is unexpected), Pictorial analogy (symbol A drawn as symbol B). Output 12-18 ideas (6 patterns x 2-3). Use first in any session.
- SCAMPER: Substitute, Combine, Adapt, Modify or magnify, Put to other use, Eliminate, Reverse (the men's product addressed to women). Two answers per operator, even forced.
- TRIZ for ads: state the contradiction ("need X but Y blocks it") and apply Segmentation, Extraction, Local quality, Asymmetry, Merging, Universality, Nesting, Inversion (anti-advertising, admitting the flaw), Dynamicity, Copying (metaphor instead of demonstration).
- Morphological analysis: 5-7 parameters (Tone x Mechanic x Channel x Hero x Proof), 4-6 values each, draw 10-15 random combinations, keep 3-5.

**Association and collision**
- Bisociation: collide the product's world with a far donor world and find the one structural element shared by both ("you are not you when you are hungry" = hunger x celebrity persona).
- Random entry: a concrete random noun, 5-7 of its attributes, force each onto the brief (bank + aquarium -> transparency of terms).
- Forced connections: "if the product were [object], what would that mean?" 5-10 objects; keep what reads in 3 seconds.
- Synectics, four analogies: direct (where else is this problem solved?), personal (be the product: what do I fear?), symbolic (describe it with an oxymoron, "safe speed"), fantasy (no physics: how would it work?).

**Inversion and disruption**
- Reverse brainstorming: "how do we make sure nobody buys this?" 10-15 answers, invert each.
- Worst possible idea: 10 terrible ideas, list the 3-4 attributes that make each bad, invert one attribute at a time (a 24-hour watch ad -> a 1-second banner proving precision).
- Provocation PO: an absurd statement ("PO: the brand advertises a competitor"), then move from it by extraction, moment-to-moment, positive aspects, or special circumstances (under what conditions is this not absurd? when the brand is that confident).
- Oblique strategies: a cryptic card applied literally between passes ("emphasize the flaws" -> the car ad built on dents and scratches as evidence of a life).

**Volume and refinement**: Crazy 8s (8 ideas in 8 minutes, no stopping), brainwriting 6-3-5 (3 ideas evolved through 6 rounds = 18 with traceable lineage), starbursting (30+ questions on who/what/where/when/why/how; "where is insurance completely inappropriate?" -> at the roller coaster), six thinking hats (facts, feelings, risks, benefits, alternatives, process), first principles (strip conventions: "a car ad must show the car" is a convention; "people buy mobility" is the truth), design-sprint sketch (describe the idea in three frames: first second, the twist, the finale).

Rules of the generation pass: 8-12 ideas; mark the first three as warm-up (the serial-order effect makes later ideas measurably more original; bias toward ideas 5 to 12); tie every idea to a named tension; one sentence plus 2-3 lines each; check every idea for an unresolved tension (if everything resolves cleanly, originality is weak). Rotate methods between passes; never reuse a method in two consecutive passes; on a restart switch all three methods and change the altitude of the "how might we".

### 1.4 Scoring

**Gate first (pass/fail, 8 questions).** Is there an idea sayable in one sentence? Does it carry the intended message? Does it respond to the insight? Does it suit the audience? Mandatory elements present? Legal and ethical? Brand voice preserved? Backed by a real product attribute? One fail and the idea does not proceed.

**Six weighted criteria (1-10 each).**
```
Score = Originality x 0.25 + Strategic fit x 0.20 + Emotional response x 0.20
      + Feasibility x 0.15 + Scalability x 0.10 + Simplicity x 0.10
```
Calibration anchors:
- Originality 1-2 pure category template; 4-5 expected twist, viewer guesses the payoff; 6-7 a competitor could not put its logo on it; 8-9 nothing like it in the category, retold a week later; 10 creates a genre others copy for years.
- Strategic fit 6-7 clearly on brief; 8-9 on brief and adds what the client needed but did not ask for; 10 redefines the task.
- Emotional response is tiered. Tier 1 generic (happy, sad, angry, afraid): score at most 6. Tier 2 specific single emotions (nostalgic, defiant, wistful, protective, recognized, witnessed): 6-8. Tier 3 two feelings in unresolved tension (bittersweet pride, ironic sincerity, wry affection, vulnerable defiance, melancholic joy, dignified grief, cathartic release, sublime terror, defensive hope, reluctant optimism): 8-10. A 9+ requires Tier 3. "Positive emotion" is not an answer.
- Feasibility 8-9 elegantly simple to execute (print names on the cans); 1-2 needs a budget 10x the brief.
- Scalability 6-7 a season across 3+ channels; 8-9 a platform for years; 10 outgrows advertising.
- Simplicity 8-9 one sentence, one image, retold in 5 seconds; 1-2 needs instructions, three steps and a QR code.

**Anti-inflation rules.** Before any 9+, name three real award-winning campaigns at that level and ask whether this stands beside them. A batch of 10 ideas should average 5-6; above 7 you are flattering. Record your gut score before analysis; if analysis raises it by more than 2 points you are rationalizing. Replace the brand with a competitor: if the idea still works, originality is at most 5. Show it mentally to a friend outside advertising: no forward, no dinner mention, score below 7. Imagine it in a "best of the year" list; if it would not be there, at most 7.

**Saturation cap.** Identify the idea's pattern (format as idea, enemy or conflict, behavior inversion, brand as activist, cultural hijack, limitation as power, invisible brand, craft as message, user as co-author, serialization and ritual, absurd as carrier, social experiment, truth telling, product as proof, benefit hyperbole, long-form drama, design as idea, tech as canvas). The crowded patterns (user as co-author, absurd as carrier, long-form drama, truth telling, craft as message) cap originality at 7 unless the mechanic is structurally new, not a topical refresh. Finish the sentence "We are doing X, which differs from prior cases because Y." If you cannot, the idea is sub-canon.

**Four-chair panel.** Score independently as creative director (would I put this in my portfolio?), strategist (does it solve the client's problem?), consumer (would I stop scrolling and show a friend?), jury (shortlist?). A gap of more than 2 points between chairs is a signal: CD high and consumer low is an industry in-joke; consumer high and jury low works but does not surprise; strategist high and CD low needs craft.

**Human-impact second scale (1-10).** 1-2 destructive or no idea, 3-4 invisible, 5 a mission people understand, 6 an intelligent idea bigger than its channel, 7 changes what people think or do with flawless craft, 8 becomes part of life, 9 changes how people live. Below 7: do not present. Gap table: craft score 8+ with human score below 7 is "clever but does not matter" (find the tension); craft below 7 with human 8+ is "matters but boring" (fix craft and surprise).

### 1.5 The "why is this not a 9" pass

For each of the top three: name every criterion below 8 and the one specific move that lifts it; apply a method from a different family than the one that produced the idea; rescore. A gain under 0.3 per pass means plateau. Stop when the top idea is 9.0+ and human impact 7+, after 5 passes (deliver the best with an honest "here is where we stopped"), or after two passes with delta under 0.2. Below 7 or plateaued: restart, and restart the insight too, not only the methods. Argue against your favorite; if the argument is stronger than the idea, the idea is weak. "Uncomfortable beats comfortable": if it makes nobody uneasy it hooks nobody. Run a pre-mortem on the finalist: imagine it launched six months ago and failed; check brand misalignment, cultural backlash, a single production dependency that collapses everything, metric mismatch (does it produce the number the client reports?), saturation; place each on probability x impact; a high x high cell with no mitigation kills the concept.

### 1.6 The stranger's sentence

Before anything is drawn, write the idea as a stranger would repeat it to a friend who has not seen it: one sentence, no execution, no adjectives, 25 words or fewer. "We ask the whole world to turn its lights off for one hour." If the sentence needs a second sentence, it is not yet an idea; it is a plan. Give the idea a name before the meeting, or the client will ("the one with the cat"). Ideas are bought in seconds or not at all. Present in the order what it is, how it works, how it looks; never open with the filter or the format. Say less than you think you need to; the best signal is the listener adding to the idea.

## 2. Hooks

### 2.1 What the first seconds must do

A hook does three jobs or the film leaks: confirm the click the title or thumbnail promised, open a question the viewer cannot close, and prove the payoff exists. A hook that only does the third is a summary, and summaries leak. Rules: confirm the title in the first sentence; one idea only (a hook carrying two promises keeps neither); specific beats big ("400 to 200,000 in seven months" beats "massive growth"); if you promised an artifact, show it inside 20 seconds; never open with who you are, the subscribe pitch is unearned.

### 2.2 The 21 hook formulas, with one example each

1. The statistic. A number the viewer did not know, stated flat, then the consequence. "97% of people who start a channel quit before video 30. Here is what the other 3% do differently." Fails when the number is vague or unsourced; say the source aloud.
2. Someone else's result. A named outcome, then the mechanism. "This channel went from 400 subscribers to 200,000 in seven months on one format. I mapped it." Never invent the number.
3. The mistake. Name the error the viewer is making right now. "Your first 30 seconds are why nobody finishes your videos. It is not the topic." Works only if the mistake is common and checkable in one second.
4. Contrarian flip. State the accepted advice, reject it. "Everyone says post daily. I posted once a week for a year and tripled the channel." Needs evidence within 15 seconds.
5. The reveal. Promise a specific thing will be shown, then show it. "I am going to show you the exact prompt, on screen, that wrote this video." A reveal at 8:00 is a retention cliff at 1:00.
6. The superlative. The single best, worst or fastest of a category. "This is the fastest way to turn one long video into thirty shorts." Earn it by naming what it beat.
7. The clock. Bound the payoff in time. "In the next six minutes you will have a title, a thumbnail and a script for your next video." Overrun it and the next hook is not believed.
8. I tried it. First-person experiment with a stated cost. "I let an AI run my channel for 30 days. I did not touch the uploads." Needs a real cost: time, money, risk.
9. The question. Ask the exact question the viewer typed. "Why do your videos die at 30 seconds when the topic is fine?" Pull it from the comments, not from your head.
10. Before and after. Two states, one cut. "This was my thumbnail. This is my thumbnail now. Same video, four times the click-through." Visual only; never on a talking head alone.
11. The teardown. Take a real thing apart in public. "I pulled the nine most-viewed videos of the month and broke down what the titles share." Name how many; a teardown of one is an anecdote.
12. The stack. Two named tools combined into one outcome. "An assistant model plus your analytics export gives you next week's upload schedule in one prompt." Both must be nameable and both necessary.
13. The warning. A cost the viewer is about to pay. "Do not upload another video until you check this one setting." If the cost is small the hook is a lie; reserve it.
14. The list. A counted set, count in the first line. "Seven things I would do differently if I started a channel this year." Exact count, each item different in kind.
15. The receipt. Show the artifact first, explain second. "This is the analytics page. This video did 40% of the channel's watch time. Here is why." Requires a real screenshot.
16. The insider. Information from inside a system. "The platform tells you which videos are underperforming. Almost nobody opens the report." Never claim access you lack.
17. The impossible claim. Something that sounds untrue, then proof. "You can write, title, thumbnail and schedule a week of videos without opening an editor." Proof starts within 20 seconds or it reads as clickbait.
18. The comparison. Two named options, one winner. "I wrote the same video three ways. One held 62%, the other two did not." Say the loser aloud; a comparison with no loser is an advert.
19. The origin. Where a result came from. "Every video on this channel starts in the same 12-line file. Here it is." Works once per channel.
20. The deadline. Something changes on a date. "The old description format stops mattering this month. Here is what replaces it." Only with a checkable date; fastest formula to lose trust with.
21. The direct address. Name the exact viewer in the first six words. "If you have under a thousand subscribers, this is the only video you need this week." Narrow beats broad; "if you make videos" addresses nobody.

Write five hooks against the formulas, score them (2.3), keep the top two, show both with their panels. Never hand over one hook.

### 2.3 The hook scoring rubric and how to score a written hook

Five properties, each 0-100. Verdict = 0.6 x mean + 0.4 x the weakest property. The weakest-link weighting is deliberate: four strong properties and one dead one is a hook that leaks at the dead one, and averaging hides it. Bands: 72+ strong, 55-71 workable, below 55 weak.

- Specificity. Start at 34. +22 per concrete token (a number, a percent, a currency amount, a count of seconds/minutes/days/weeks/months/years, a k/m/x suffix). -16 per vague booster (amazing, incredible, insane, crazy, huge, massive, game, changer, secret, powerful, ultimate, best, revolutionary, mind, blowing, unbelievable). -5 per filler word (basically, actually, literally, just, really, very, so, kind, sort, like, guys, hey, welcome, today, video, subscribe, channel). +6 per capitalized word after the first (named things), capped at +18.
- Address. Start at 26. +20 per you/your/yourself. +30 if a "you" appears within the first six words.
- Stakes. Start at 22. +26 per stake word (lose, lost, waste, wasting, quit, fail, broke, cost, risk, before, stop, never, die, dying, dead). +14 if any concrete number is present.
- Curiosity. Start at 24. +17 per gap word (why, how, what, which, until, before, but, nobody, almost, except, reason, actually). +18 if it ends with a question mark. -18 if it answers itself (because, so that, which means).
- Brevity. 9 to 24 words scores 100 (the band a spoken hook lands in at about 150 words per minute inside 10 seconds). Under 9: 100 minus 11 per missing word, floor 30. Over 24: 100 minus 7 per extra word, floor 10.

Fix for the weakest property: specificity, swap one adjective for a number, a name or a date; address, say "you" in the first six words; stakes, name what it costs them to keep doing it the current way; curiosity, cut the half of the sentence that answers itself; brevity, read it aloud and stop where you run out of breath.

Honesty about the instrument: measured against 74 real short-form openings, this separates deliberately bad hooks from real ones well and separates a creator's own hits from their own misses barely at all. A low score is a reason to look again; a high score is not a promise. A hook that matches no formula is usually a summary, not a hook.

### 2.4 First-three-seconds rules for video

In video a hook is three simultaneous components, not a line: visual action (what is literally happening on screen in seconds 0-3, its job is to stop the thumb), spoken line (the first words, its job is to open the loop), caption text (on-screen, its job is to anchor the claim for sound-off viewers). No-duplication rule: the three must complement, never repeat. If the voice says "I stopped paying 200 a month for my gym" while the caption reads the same words over a static face, two of three slots are wasted; split the work: the visual shows the cancellation email, the voice says the line, the caption names the alternative. A hook spec with one column filled is a third of a hook. Statics collapse to two components (visual + headline); the headline must not caption the image.

Opening moves to cycle through: curiosity gap (withhold the noun, must pay off inside the ad), bold falsifiable claim (needs substantiation on screen), first-person confession (reads fake without lived-in detail), contrast or before/after (must be visually honest), hyper-specific POV ("it is 3 p.m. and you are on your fourth coffee"; generic POV is invisible), the buyer's question verbatim, countdown or on-screen challenge (payoff must exist), proof-first (lead with the receipt; strongest when the proof brags by itself).

Generation pipeline, top down: Segment (a slice with a shared situation, from reviews and comments, not the whole audience) -> Motivation (one pain, desire or objection in their words, verbatim) -> Format (street interview, POV selfie, screen recording, unboxing, side-by-side, text-on-screen static, founder to camera, reaction stitch; pick before writing the line) -> Hook (three components). Output a matrix: segment | motivation (verbatim source) | format | visual action | spoken line | caption. Ten hooks for ten cells beat thirty rewordings of one.

Hook slop to delete: a generic "POV:", "stop scrolling", "here is what nobody tells you about X". Earn the next second with something specific to this viewer.

### 2.5 Retention beats across a video

The retention graph is the only honest feedback. It shows three different problems with three different fixes:
- Hook leak: what is lost in the first 30 seconds. Under 25% is healthy, 25-40% leaking, over 40% severe. Always fixed first, always in the first fifteen seconds of script, never in the edit.
- Cliffs: single steep drops (steeper than about 0.8% between adjacent samples). A cliff is a moment: a topic change with no signpost, a sponsor read, a long setup. Read what was being said at that second and change that.
- Slide: the steady bleed across the middle. A flat slide is pacing; the fix is cutting, not rewriting.

Beat structure of a video that holds:
- 0:00-0:15 hook (2.1 to 2.4).
- 0:15-0:45 the turn: say in one sentence what the video is going to do and start doing it. No channel intro, no "before we get started", no subscribe pitch; these are the most common cause of the cliff at 0:30.
- Body: one idea per beat; mark on every beat what is ON SCREEN, not only what is said. A talking head with nothing to look at is a podcast. Add a new fact at each mid-script turn; signpost every topic change.
- Payoff: deliver what the hook promised and say that you are delivering it ("that is the prompt, it is in the description").
- Close: one ask, not three.
Runtime estimate at 150 words per minute. Name the single biggest leak and one change for it, not a list of five.

For a short cut from a long video: pick a 20-55 second span that opens on a complete thought, contains a turn (a claim, then something that complicates or proves it), and ends on a line rather than a trail-off; write a new first line (the original assumes context this viewer lacks), on-screen text for the first two seconds in different words from the spoken line, and a loop point where the last line sets up the first.

## 3. Writing lines people say

### 3.1 Dialogue is action

Every line is an action a character takes to get something from someone. Three questions before any line: what does the character want right now, what action gets it, what words execute that action? Tag the action beneath each line with a gerund phrase ("groveling at her feet", "ignoring his plea"). A line with no answer to "what is the real action in the subtext?" is not finished. Silence is an action; a pause is a reaction.

Three layers of content: the said (vocabulary, syntax, tone: this is characterization), the unsaid (consciously hidden thought and feeling, which the viewer must be able to sense), the unsayable (the unconscious need that only shows under pressure, in a choice). Lines characterize; actions reveal true character. The more external and social the conflict, the fewer the words; the more private and personal, the more.

The best dialogue is often none. A woman takes off her bracelet, her necklace, stops at the ring; the man just looks at her; she sighs and takes off the ring. Before writing a scene ask "how would I shoot this with no lines at all?"

### 3.2 Subtext

Write the subtext under every line or cut the line: if a line does not imply a thought or feeling beneath its surface, enrich it or kill it. "Written on the nose" means the subtext has become the text; a candlelit "I love you, I love you" meant sincerely is unplayable. Two legitimate on-the-nose exceptions: "what do you want?" and "are you all right?". Non-realist genres (myth, fairy tale, animation, fable) may run thinner subtext; realism never.

Design the sentence so the key word lands last: the suspense sentence ("so why did you give me that... look? gun? kiss?") holds its core to the final word and launches the other character's reaction; the cumulative sentence puts the core first and feels conversational; the balanced sentence puts it in the middle. Mix them; any one shape repeated becomes wallpaper. Highlight the last word of every speech; it should be the word the next character reacts to.

Argument over agreement: let every line challenge the one before it. Two people agreeing is dead air. Small talk only when it transcends itself and connects to the theme.

Two-handers go stiff; give the pair a third thing to fight through (the canary, the wine, the chess game, the film they both saw). The third thing carries what neither can say ("this grape only grows in one small corner of the world and needs constant care... it will not come to you, you have to go to it").

### 3.3 No exposition

Release the least information possible, as late as possible. List every fact the audience needs, rank by importance, repeat the important ones. Rules:
- Dramatize, do not narrate. "We have known each other twenty years since school" becomes "you have not changed since you were the immature punk from school, smell that shirt"; the audience now knows "twenty years" and "school" without being told.
- Exposition as ammunition: the character uses what they know as a weapon in the fight ("I am your father" disarms the son at the climax). Information that is thrown, not offered.
- Never have one character tell another what both already know.
- The most powerful revelation is a secret from the past, saved for the moment the character must choose the lesser evil. Backstory is not biography; it is the one past event that turns the scene.
- Put unavoidable explanation inside an unexpected situation (the argument about wedding seating happens during the jewelry heist).
- Delete anything common sense could infer.
Exercise: rewrite a block of hard exposition three ways: as a weapon thrown, inside a scene designed to coax it out, hidden so the behavior stays believable without it.

### 3.4 The scene's value turn

A scene exists only if a value at stake changes charge between its first and last line. Write the value and its charge at the top (love, negative) and at the bottom (love, more negative, or positive); if identical, ask why the scene exists; the answer is almost always "exposition", so cut it and weave the facts elsewhere.

Five steps: (1) define the conflict, who drives, what they want in a goal phrase, what the opposing force wants, directly opposed, not tangential; (2) note the opening value charge; (3) break the scene into beats, one beat = one action/reaction, a new beat only when behavior visibly changes; (4) note the closing charge and compare; (5) inspect the beats for the arc: each must build on the previous until the turning point, which is created only by an action or a revelation and produces surprise, curiosity, insight, new direction in that order.

Each character carries a scene intention (what they want now; when it is satisfied the scene stops), a super-intention (the unconscious want, often the opposite of how they present), and a background desire (the net of constraints that limits what can be said). The earliest sign of a badly written scene is repeated beats: the same tactic in different words (plead, refuse, plead, refuse). The first ice cream is good, the third makes you sick; three crying scenes make the third one a comedy. Four scene diseases: desire without opposition, loops without progress, a turning point too early or too late, text where subtext should be. Asymmetric conflict is a legitimate shape (one presses, one evades) as are indirect (polite words hiding contempt), reflexive (a character against themselves), and minimal (confession in place of argument, pauses opening doors for thought).

### 3.5 Enter late, leave early

Enter the scene at the last possible moment and leave before the conflict fully resolves; the unresolved feeling carries tension into the next scene. Cut from the peroxide in the bathroom to the bed afterward. Applies to a scene and to a single line (go straight to the main course). Average scene 2.5 minutes, 40-60 scenes in a feature; shorten scenes approaching a climax to earn the right to the slow one. The big reversal scene itself is long, slow, tense.

Transitions: the third element shared or opposed between two adjacent scenes: a character trait (a playful child -> a childish adult), an action, an object (greenhouse -> jungle), a line, the quality of light (dawn shadow -> sunset glow), a sound (waves -> snoring), an idea. Name it, do not hope for it.

Action over talk: anything a character does beats nothing. The camera's great power is enlarging the small: a wedding ring slipping off and rolling under a table of legs; french toast made badly at the start and like a chef at the end; a pen drawing ink. Avoid phones, cars, restaurants, offices and dinner tables unless the place becomes the center of the action; list a dozen alternative locations for every scene and pick the one that makes the conflict harder and the character more exposed (the police chief swims laps on doctor's orders while the detective shouts from the poolside and chlorine splashes 600-dollar shoes). Convert states to doing: "I am sad" -> "I weep" -> the son mocks or comforts. Write reactions against expectation (the hero facing the swordsman draws a pistol). Play anger with a smile; talk about flowers while falling in love; plan the robbery in the pool hall while the police walk through.

Suspense is not secrecy. You cannot keep a secret from the audience; the lasting pleasure is foreknowledge: the audience knows the news before the birthday feast and watches the family force smiles. Set one suspense while dissolving the last; delay before release (the crouch before the jump), and build three layers of false alarm before the real one. The deepest suspense is about character and fate, not plot facts.

### 3.6 Credibility flaws and how to fix them

Credibility is not realism; conversation wastes words, dialogue concentrates meaning. Flaws and fixes:
- Empty words (telling what the other knows): make it ammunition or cut it.
- Over-emoting (language far beyond the feeling): the true problem of melodrama is not too much expression but too little motivation. Match motivation to action, then ask direct or oblique; looking at your nails and whispering "crucify him" beats roaring it.
- Over-knowing (the author's knowledge in the character's mouth) and over-perceiving (a character describing themselves more deeply than a psychoanalyst could): give the character an excuse, not the motive; motive is unconscious, the line is the excuse ("because you are too beautiful, I killed him").
- Cliche: write the first word that comes, then improvise more and wilder, choose, discard the rest; nobody needs to see your second choice.
- Character-neutral language: "oh my God" becomes "sweet bleeding Jesus" in one region, "Lord have mercy" in another, "well, damn me to hell" in a third.
- Ornamented language (shouting "see how clever my dialogue is"): when in doubt, lower the volume.
- Dry language, eight fixes: concrete over abstract; familiar over strange; short words over long ("his rendering was a falsification of the facts" -> "he lied"; the more agitated, active or afraid, the shorter the words; the more learned, the longer); direct over roundabout; active over passive ("she is playing around" -> "she plays around"); short sentences over long; expressive over imitative ("do not imitate life, express it"); cut hi, oh, well, that, also.
- Inner monologue fallacy: a long uninterrupted speech aimed at nobody becomes the author's mouthpiece; break it into action and reaction, insert the listener's silent responses; the talker may exhaust the other characters but never the audience.
- Missed cue: the line's final word should be the cue for the reaction.
- Poor design: a scene that forks (subtext and text disagree because the inner life is flat, or the lines overstate it, or the two are unrelated, or there is no conflict). Rewriting trap: the more you revise lines, the more literal they become, until the subtext is gone; the fix is in the event and the character, not the words.

Character-specific voice: the cover-the-names test. Hide the speaker names and ask whether you can still tell who speaks; if everyone talks like the writer, fail. Vocabulary betrays character more than anything: nouns and verbs show the range of knowledge ("a big nail" vs "a spike, a tack, a brad, a peg"; "walked slowly across the room" vs "drifted, waltzed, slunk, dragged himself, strutted"); modifiers, voice and mood show personality ("real big" vs "magnificent"; "the family planned the wedding" vs "the wedding was planned by the family"; "could plan" vs "must be planned by"). Give each character a cultural reference as a third thing. The inverse test for stock speeches: hand the speech to another character; if it still fits, it is a slogan, not a person.

Comedy dialogue: comic characters are driven by a blind obsession and are rigid; empathy is death for the comic writer, the audience needs cool distance; minor characters get one trait each. Four techniques: clarity (the fewest, clearest words), exaggeration (small things made huge or huge made small), timing (setup then punch, the punch on the last word and nothing after it; never step on your own joke), incongruity (dignified adults fighting like children). Comedy escalates down a spiral: personality -> appearance -> emotion -> violence. Jokes sit just before or just after the point of highest tension.

Format rules: no "I think", "you know", "listen", "look"; no phonetic dialect; no underlining, no exclamation marks, at most two ellipses (and only when one person starts a sentence and the other finishes it); almost no parenthetical acting directions (the actor who says "you want the keys? here, take them" brightly and sweetly reveals more sickness than "(furious)" ever would). Writers are not paid by the word ("what was your childhood like?" "short").

Diagnostic list before any line ships: the gerund under the line; the cover-the-names test; anybody telling anybody what both know; anything on the nose outside the two exceptions; a stiff two-hander without a third thing; a beat repeated; the last word of each speech is the cue; a long speech where every sentence gives new information; anywhere two people agree; whether the scene could be done without words; whether the melodrama is really a motivation gap.

## 4. Dramaturgy for generated video

### 4.1 The scene formula

```
Scene = hero's desire + obstacle + space geometry + controlled gaze + editing rhythm
```
Desire: what the character wants in this specific second. Obstacle: object, person, fear, distance, rule. Space geometry: who stands where, who has the power position, which direction is threat and which is escape. Controlled gaze: one focal point per frame, where the eye is forced to land. Editing rhythm: how long each shot lives, where the pause lands, where the cut bites. Name all five in one sentence each before writing a prompt; if you cannot, the scene is not ready and no prompt will save it.

Blocking is the choreography of desire: for each character, what they want, whom they move toward, away from, corner, yield to, and which gesture leaks the hidden want. Not "he stands near the window" but "he edges toward the window with his shoulder still angled back toward her, as if the conversation holds him." Staging states the conflict before a word: the standing character dominates the seated one; the one in the doorway controls the room; the one behind glass or in reflection is psychologically distant; shadow carries threat or grief; negative space is isolation; tight framing is suffocation; a shared frame without eye contact is broken intimacy. Name the power dynamic the staging reveals.

Camera must have a reason: every move answers "what changed?" If nothing changed, the camera is static. Legitimate reasons: a decision was made, new information entered, pressure rose and the frame tightens, the character looked and the camera reveals what they saw, a gesture pulled focus, the space changed. "Push-in starts on 'I don't know' and stops on her jaw locking" is a direction; "cinematic gliding camera" is not.

Spatial clarity beats montage hysteria: even in chaos the viewer must know where the hero is, where the threat is, which way is escape, which way is decision. Sketch the geography in one sentence before any fast cut ("hero moves left to right; threat enters from frame top; exit is off right"). Environment plays: one environmental pressure per scene carries the emotion: flickering fluorescent = decay and dread, rain on one window = withheld grief, kettle steam = suppressed anger, buzzing AC = dissociation, wet night asphalt = guilt, tight corridor = walls closing, mirror or glass = self-reckoning, cold overhead office light = judgment.

### 4.2 Three details per shot

The most violated law. Every shot owns three concrete physical details, or it is filler:
1. One environmental pressure: a physical fact of the space that carries the feeling (cold blue refrigerator light, steam off boiling water, wet asphalt, a dripping tap, a curtain breathing in the air conditioning).
2. One physical micro-action on the body: the emotion translated into muscle (jaw locks, knuckles whiten on the fork, lips press flat, eyes drop a quarter inch, a hard swallow, fingers curl against the doorframe). The body is the only place a feeling renders; names of feelings do not. For one emotional transition, 2-4 observable cues (eyes, brow, mouth, breath, throat, hands); fewer leaves the model guessing, more reads as overacting.
3. One sound anchor or recurring visual motif tied to the spine: a stomach growl at 2.3 seconds, the reflection in a dark phone screen on every transition, the same fluorescent flicker before every cut, the clock's second hand.

Zero details is filler, one is thin, three is a shot. No exemptions for "establishing", "transition" or "hero product" shots; those are the ones that go lazy first, and one thin shot drags the whole sequence into mush.

Banned words (each is a placeholder for a missing detail): cinematic, professional, high quality, masterpiece, stunning, epic, amazing, beautiful lighting, dynamic camera, intense moment, powerful scene, dramatic close-up, "he is sad", "she is angry", "he is afraid". Replace each with physical facts.

Three-jobs rule: every shot changes emotion, advances action (new physical event, information or position), or increases pressure (stakes, clock, tightening space, a witness). A shot that does none is a wrapper with no candy; delete it. A beautiful establishing shot is not a job. A hero shot of the product is not a job.

Five anchors per piece, exactly five: one main emotion, one visual motif, one anchor object, one break, one final image. Example: guilt; the hero reflected in glass surfaces; a phone with one unread message; he deletes the message; his face ghosted in the dark phone screen. The final image is non-negotiable in every clip: the model uses the ending as its emotional destination ("ends on his face frozen in the blue fridge light" beats "he stands there sadly").

### 4.3 Murch's six rules

The priority order for where to cut. Each item outweighs everything below it combined.
1. Emotion, 51%: does the cut honor the emotional truth of the moment; what does the viewer feel now and what should they feel next.
2. Story, 23%: does the cut advance story or reveal character.
3. Rhythm, 10%: does the cut fall on the scene's musical beat.
4. Eye-trace, 7%: where is the viewer's gaze at the cut, and does the new shot receive it.
5. Two-dimensional plane, 5%: does the cut respect screen direction.
6. Three-dimensional space, 4%: does the cut respect the real geometry of the location.
Cutting for pace alone is item 3; done without 1 and 2 it produces an attention-deficit advert.

### 4.4 The 14-field shot card

Fill every field for every shot. An empty field is a missing direction; fill it or drop the shot.
1. Shot ID: 01, 02, 03.
2. Beat: what changes in the story here, in one clause.
3. Emotion: fear, shame, anger, guilt, resolve, relief; the one feeling this shot carries.
4. Frame: wide, medium, close-up, macro insert, over-the-shoulder, POV.
5. Composition: center, edge, negative space, reflection, silhouette, foreground obstruction.
6. Camera: static, push-in, handheld, tracking, whip-pan; one dominant move.
7. Movement reason: the answer to "what changed?" that justifies the move; "none, static" is a valid answer.
8. Action: the exact physical event, playable frame by frame.
9. Eye trace: where the viewer's gaze lands in the first 0.3 seconds.
10. Duration: 0.5 s, 1 s, 3 s.
11. Cut type: match cut, smash cut, cut on action, J-cut, L-cut.
12. Sound: breath, bass hit, street wash, phone ring, silence.
13. Light and color: source, direction, quality, concrete palette (cold blue-gray, crushed blacks, one amber practical).
14. Production note: prop, location, actor or reference-image direction, continuity anchor.

Shot functions to tag each card with: Establish (where), Reveal (what enters), Power (who controls the room), Pressure (what pushes), Detail (object, hand, eye, receipt, door; the anchor object usually lives here), Reaction (face after the event), Shift (inner change made visible), Impact (the decisive frame), Aftermath (the residue), Exit (the image carried out). A full scene usually runs Establish -> Power -> Pressure -> Detail -> Reaction -> Shift -> Impact -> Aftermath -> Exit. Two adjacent shots with the same function get averaged by the model into one frame; vary function and framing at every cut.

For a still keyframe (an animatic panel) add three fields: implied camera (the move this still is the first frame of), emotion via object (the feeling a face would show, mapped to an object's state: a tach needle pinned in red, knuckles whitening, a sidewall folding), and motion as still (which frozen cue carries the missing movement: directional background smear with sharp subject, one partially blurred element, a streak frame, a posture that can only resolve forward, sharp subject against dissolved field, or deliberate zero-blur stillness). Objects carry feeling only when they visibly change state: board the after (ignition lamp blooming, slick mid-fold), the before is implied.

### 4.5 Rhythm ladders and the beat map

Rhythm is stepped, never uniform. Always one pause before the biggest cut; the pause before the impact matters more than the speed of the cuts, and without it speed is a meat grinder.
- Slow-burn drama: 4 s, 4 s, 3 s, 2 s, 1 s, pause, 2 s.
- Commercial product arc: 3 s, 2 s, 1.5 s, 1 s, 0.5 s (product macro), 2 s (hero shot).
- Anxiety build: 2 s, 1 s, 1 s, 0.5 s, 0.5 s, 0.3 s, pause, 1 s.
- Impact scene: pause, 0.2 s flash, 2 s aftermath in stillness.
- The staircase inside any 8-10 second montage: long, shorter, shorter, pause, impact (4 s wide, 2 s medium, 1 s eyes, 12 frames macro insert, 8 frames hand, 6 frames detail cue, 2 s sudden silence, 1 s decision).

Beat map for 60-90 seconds (compress proportionally for 30 or 15; never skip the Crack or the Impact):
```
0-5 s    Hook         hero already in tension, no setup, problem on screen
5-15 s   Context      where, who is near, what is at stake
15-30 s  Pressure     hero tries to hold control
30-45 s  Crack        a detail breaks the hero's position
45-60 s  Acceleration cuts shorten, breath tightens
60-75 s  Impact       decision, break, confession, action
75-90 s  Aftermath    brief silence or visual residue
```
Choose the story arc before laying beats. Causal chain (ordinary state -> disruption -> consequence -> consequence -> resolution) for quests and tasks; turn without conflict (setup -> widening -> an unrelated element recontextualizes -> reconciliation) for discoveries and found objects with no antagonist; one peak moment (just enough context -> the peak -> the residue) for vows, farewells, the winning second, where Crack and Impact coincide and Aftermath carries most of the time, and no fake obstacle is manufactured; conflict beat sheet (opening image -> inciting event -> midpoint reversal -> lowest point -> climax -> closing image) when two forces oppose and the outcome is in doubt; hook and open end for episode one and teasers. Two plausible arcs means the brief is undecided; running two at once makes a film that changes its mind.

Narrative frameworks mapped to ad lengths: story spine (once upon a time, every day, but one day, because of that x3, until finally, ever since) runs lines 3-7 only at 15 s and the full eight at 30 s; sparkline (what is / what could be alternating, then the new reality) takes one switch at 15 s, two at 30 s, three or four at 60 s; a five-act arc puts the climax at 8-10 s of a 15, at 18-22 s of a 30; the motivated sequence for direct response is attention -> need -> satisfaction -> visualization -> action, with visualization owning up to 15-20 s of a 60; in the hero's journey the customer is the hero and the brand is the guide, never the hero.

### 4.6 One beat per three seconds

Pacing density by genre, per 5-second generation: emotional drama 3-4 beats with longer reactions; standard narrative 4-7; fast montage or music video 6-9. More than 9 beats in 5 seconds is incoherent motion on any model. In a 30-second single-pass generation, time windows of 3 seconds or more execute reliably, shorter ones do not; one core action and one camera move per window; a time range is a budget, not an edit point. Loudness equals shot count: a silent beat gets one held panel of 1.5-2.5 s, a bang gets a 6-9 panel burst at 8 to 4 frames each, and no two bursts touch; a held quiet panel separates every burst. A 30-second spot lands at about 18-26 panels, plotted as a descending duration staircase broken only by the one pre-impact pause and the one post-impact hold. End on consequence, not explanation.

Montage patterns to fill: escalation (wide -> medium -> close -> macro -> reaction -> impact); anxiety (face -> object -> hand -> face -> object closer -> sound cue -> sudden stillness); discovery (POV -> empty space -> searching hand -> hidden object -> rack focus -> reaction); catastrophe (anticipation -> instability -> reaction -> fall -> impact -> silence -> collapse; tiny object failures play bigger than explosions); product drama (lifestyle setup -> reveal -> macro texture -> human reaction -> use moment -> hero shot); music-video loop (gesture A -> new angle of A -> transformation -> A again -> release).

Dramaturgy check before any prompt leaves: scene formula complete; three details on every shot; three-jobs on every shot; a reason for every camera move; readable geometry; five anchors named. Step two is the one everybody skips.

## 5. Prompt syntax per video model

### 5.1 Rules that hold on every model

- Skeleton and order: subject and character, action, scene, camera and lens, lighting, style and palette, sound, duration and ratio, continuity rules, negatives only where the model honors them. Generators weight the first 30-40% of tokens; lead with subject and action, put style last.
- Full sentences, not tag soup; brief a human cinematographer. "Masterpiece, 4k, cinematic" degrades video output.
- One dominant camera move per 5-second shot, one subtle secondary at most. Say the lens: 24 mm immersive wide, 35 mm documentary, 50 mm intimate, 85 mm compressed portrait, 100 mm macro texture, anamorphic 40 mm widescreen. Rare terms need a translation to visible change: "rack focus: the leaves in the foreground blur as her face sharpens."
- Identity block at the start of every prompt and repeated verbatim in every clip of a sequence: face shape, eye color, skin tone, hair color/length/style, facial hair, exact garments, accessories. Generators have no memory between generations; brief each clip as a brilliant intern with memory damage.
- No contradictions ("still pond" + "flowing water"; "close-up" + "wide landscape"); the model obeys the strongest signal and smears the rest.
- Consequence prompting: describe what the action does to the world (tires kick up water curtains, the lid actually unscrews, the impact spider-webs the surface). Action chains double as QA: a broken chain exposes a failed generation at a glance.
- Duration discipline: most models work in 5-10 second clips; 30 s = 6 x 5 s, 60 s = 12 x 5 s, stitched in the edit; clip N+1 opens on the pose, light and color temperature that clip N closed on. Exceptions: Seedance multi-shot (2-3 shots in one 5-10 s clip), Kling 3.0 (up to 6 shots in one 15 s generation), Seedance 2.5 (30 s in one pass).
- Reference role discipline: every attached asset gets a written role ("@Image 1 defines the woman's face, hair and coat; do not use its background"); bind subjects one by one, never "images 1 to 4 define four characters respectively"; do not restate what a reference already defines; more assets is not more control, unassigned assets bleed into the frame.
- Priority declaration for overloaded prompts: name the subject that must survive every shot, the shots that must appear, the transitions where the model may freestyle, the mandatory final frame. Shorter with priorities beats longer without.
- Image-to-video on every model: do not re-describe what is in the image; describe only motion, camera, light change and audio; add "maintain the subject from the first frame".
- Avoid "hyperrealistic 8k masterpiece"; it pushes faces into the AI-art look. Use production language: "shot on 50 mm, natural skin texture, motivated light, documentary feel."

### 5.2 Seedance 1.x and 2.0

Unique strength: several distinct shots in one generation. Specs: 1.0 Pro 1080p, 5-10 s (API to 12); 1.0 Lite 720p; 1.5 Pro adds native audio and lip-sync; 2.0 adds 9 camera presets, up to 12 reference files (9 images, 3 videos, 3 audio), native 4K and 10-bit; 2.0 Mini about 2x faster and 30% cheaper for drafts. Ratios 16:9, 4:3, 1:1, 3:4, 9:16, 21:9, 9:21. 24-30 fps. On a 2.0 pipeline, faces are filtered aggressively; route face-heavy drama to 1.5 Pro, 2.5, Kling or Veo and keep 2.0 for scenes, architecture, product and montage.

CLI tail at the end of the prompt (1.x and 2.0 only): `--resolution 1080p --duration 5 --camerafixed false --seed 42`. Duration 2-12 on Pro; camerafixed true locks the frame.

Multi-shot markers inside one prompt: `Shot 1.` / `Cut to.` / `Camera cut to.` / `Camera switching.` / `Lens switch to.`, or inline `[A] -> Cut to -> [B] -> Camera cut to -> [C]`. 2-3 shots per 5 s for tight cinema, 4-5 short beats at most; hard cap 5 shots per generation; size duration to shot count (4 shots need 10-15 s). Every shot must share an anchor with its neighbor (same character, location or lighting recipe) or the output is disconnected clips. Default 5-second scaffold: 0.0-0.8 establish (extreme close-up or insert), 0.8-1.6 action (medium), 1.6-2.5 turn (new framing, POV, OTS, rack focus), 2.5-3.6 reaction (tight, slow push-in), 3.6-5.0 climax or hero (low angle, final image).

The 11-block production skeleton for anything dramatic or character-locked: [1] character lock with `@img1` plus the full textual identity block (the image alone drifts) and "no extra characters"; [2] length, genre, editing intent ("5-second multi-shot tragicomic sequence, fast staircase editing"); [3] story paragraph in present tense naming the break point; [4] visual style with palette, grain, what to avoid as a positive; [5] camera style (camera body look, lenses with purpose, handheld or static, "strict cuts, no continuous take"); [6] editing style (hard vs match cuts, where the pause lands, where the impact hits); [7] audio in order (ambient, micro-actions, impact, silence, final cue; "no dialogue, no subtitles, no on-screen text" when wanted); [8] shot-by-shot timeline with timecodes; [9] lighting recap (key, fill, rim, direction, temperature, what it carries psychologically); [10] composition across shots and the named final image; [11] output specs and the CLI tail.

Anti-mush guard, placed at the very top when the model smears shots into one take or drifts identity: "Important direction: this must be a clearly edited multi-shot sequence with visible cuts. Do not generate a single continuous take. Each shot has a different camera angle and framing. Rapid montage pacing with rhythmic escalation. Keep the same clothing, face, body type, facial hair and hairstyle in every shot. The tone is [genre]." The highest-leverage paragraph in any Seedance prompt.

Negatives: 1.0 has no negative parser; 2.0's is fragile. Invert to positives ("cold blue-gray palette with desaturated skin" instead of "no yellow"; "anatomically correct hands with clear finger separation" instead of "no distorted hands"). Audio (1.5+): write it in the body as a sound brief; dialogue in double quotes with a delivery note on 1.5/2.0; keep lines short or sync drifts; write "no music" explicitly or an ad-style score appears under everything. Camera presets: dolly in/out, pan, tilt, tracking, crane/aerial, handheld, zoom, Hitchcock zoom, static via camerafixed.

Failures: motion ignored under 5 s (minimum 5 s for multiple actions); one take instead of montage (add cut markers and the guard); character drift (repeat the identity block at every shot boundary); mood smear (adjacent shots sharing a function get averaged; vary function and framing).

### 5.3 Seedance 2.5

What changes: 30 s native single pass (duration 4-30), extension to 60 s in 4-30 s passes, Ultra Long mode 30-180 s in one submission; 50 references (30 images each at most 4K and 30 MB, 10 videos 2-30 s each and at most 30 s total, 10 audio clips at most 30 s total, audio-only input allowed); realistic humans with real pores and lip-sync in 11 languages are the headline, the 2.0 face caution does not apply; negatives are reliable ("pure video, no subtitles, no background music" actually suppresses them); timestamps honored to the second; editing and extension are first-class modes; output 480p/720p on the web app. Duration, ratio and resolution are set on the page or in the API and do not belong in the prompt (except Ultra Long, which restates duration and ratio at the top). The CLI tail is not 2.5 syntax.

Base formula: "<Subject> performs <primary action> in <scene>. The visuals feature <style>. Use <shot size, angle, movement or cuts>. Audio includes <dialogue, ambience, SFX or music>." Macro formula for long or reference-heavy work: [reference declaration] + [one-line summary] + [plot by timeline, each beat with positive content and local bans] + [global tail restating the must-hold globals and repeating the bans]. Re-mention the same asset several times; repetition raises accuracy.

Markers: music in `( )`, sound effects in `< >`, dialogue in `{ }`, on-screen titles and subtitles in the app's own double-bracket title marker (a pair of hollow lenticular brackets; copy it from the official guide, it has no ASCII equivalent). Do not describe audio in loose prose when a marker exists. Dialogue formula: language + regional variety + delivery + speaker + {line} ("Dialogue language: authentic Los Angeles English. The young man says in natural LA vernacular: {No way, you actually made it.}"). Lip shape and rhythm follow the assigned language, mixed-language scenes included.

Reference binding: "@Image 1 defines <subject>'s appearance, clothing, structure or material. @Video 1 defines motion, camera movement or pacing. @Audio 1 defines <character>'s voice, dialogue, ambience or music." Bind each subject individually; add exclusions ("do not use the image background", "do not use the people in the image"); never restate what a reference defines; use a subject profile block for a recurring character (appearance from @Image 1, fixed prop from @Image 5, motion from @Video 1, "do not give this character <prop>"); select references per scene ("Scene 1 | Use: ... Event: ... End state: ..."). The same character in two states needs two slots bound to two time ranges; it is the flakiest binding, budget re-rolls. Stability sweet spots: 1-5 subjects per reference video or audio, 5-10 s per subject, 1-8 subjects across images, multi-view sheets only up to 5 subjects, edit sources at most 20 s.

Stages and end states for 30 s: "[Generation Goal] ... [Stage 1] Initial state ... Primary event: one action. End state: visible state. [Stage 2] Continue from the previous stage: what stays unchanged ... [Maintain Consistency] identity, count, clothing, prop ownership, spatial direction, audio relationships." Windows of 3 s or more, consecutive and non-overlapping, one core action plus one camera move each; second precision only at handoffs, entrances, transitions, beat hits; relative triggers work ("three seconds after she presses the button, the lights go out"; "only when he says {now}"). Too little content in a window gives the model freedom; too much over-cuts and omits.

The three-module anti-collapse skeleton: a one-line logline; [Module 1 reference layer] strict face and composition locks; [Module 2 global settings] environment and texture ("emphasize extreme realistic physical texture"), film look, camera language, character styling, the performance register for the whole piece, and a specific prohibition list naming this scene's collapse modes ("no exaggerated crying, no fast cuts, no large body movements, no extra dialogue, no BGM, no runny nose, no premature tears"); [Module 3 timestamped storyboard] "[start-end s] [beat name]: physical directives + emotional subtext." The subtext line ("she is not complaining; she is waiting for him to confirm an answer she already guessed") makes the model act better.

Realistic human formula: [age / ethnicity] + [skin tone / texture] + [3-4 facial details: eye shape, brow, nose bridge, lips, jaw] + [gaze, what the eyes carry] + [hair] + [clothing / fabric] + [build / aura]. Force-append "retaining real fine pores and skin texture"; it is the official anti-plastic-skin fix. Camera terms understood without translation: extreme wide to extreme close-up; push in, pull out, pan, lateral move, follow, orbit, dive, tilt, handheld shake; low angle, overhead, first-person; one-take, dolly zoom, aerial, FPV, bullet time, bounce speed ramp. Transitions: natural cut, fade, dissolve, white or black flash, wipe, occlusion mask, match cut, action or whip cut, motion relay, zoom-through, ink-wash dissolve; always attach "prohibit rigid cutting, prohibit objects appearing out of thin air"; transitions can be delegated ("choose the most suitable from natural cut / occlusion mask / match cut for this film's style"); gate late effects on a trigger ("the ink-wash appears only after 25 seconds, on the click").

Physics: declare the regime up front ("rain on metal, tire spray and exhaust refraction must obey real-world physics"), narrate consequences, negatively constrain the known failure ("no soft-body twisting of the mecha, maintain metallic rigidity"), gate events on triggers.

Editing an existing video: "[Edit Goal] Edit @Video 1. Within <range>, <add/remove/replace/adjust> <object, region or audio category>. [Source Video Role] @Video 1 is the sole editing master; it defines characters, scene, actions, composition, camera, occlusion, audio and event order. [Target Material Role] @Image 1 defines the replacement's attributes. [Edit Scope] Modify only <target>. [Content to Preserve] Keep <everything else> from @Video 1." Subject swaps add a timeline-inheritance block (the new subject inherits every appearance, motion, occlusion and exit of the old, with timing). The longer the keep-list (identity, hair, skin, lip movement, original voice, speech rhythm, camera position, focal length, aspect, floor perspective, total duration), the less drift. Anchor edits to speech, not seconds, when the source has narration. Slice sources to 20 s. Audio-only edits and localization re-dubs ("replace the narration with fluent Spanish and the presenter with a Spanish woman, keep camera, blocking, pacing, scene and product") work.

Extension: "Extend @Video 1 forward. The first frame of the extension continues directly from the last frame of @Video 1. Maintain pose, prop position, background, camera position, lighting, motion direction. Then <new content>. Keep each subject as the same continuous instance; do not duplicate or split it." Backward extension defines @Video 1's first frame as the explicit end state and flags "<X> must not appear early" or later characters leak into the past. Minimal proven extension prompt: "Extend the video. Keep identity, facial structure, body proportions, lighting, art style and the space unchanged. Only change camera movement." Ultra Long carries the last ~3 s of each segment into the next, so a weak middle propagates forward; plan pacing with timestamps or a beat chain, or the back half drifts; segment reshoot (regenerate only a bad span) is the cheapest fix.

Blockout: a gray-model 3D previz or green-screen plate locks space, camera and staging; map every primitive to a reference ("the tall cylinder in @Video 1 is the guide; the rectangular block is the display cart; do not use its gray geometry or empty scene"); no limbed or winged models in coarse blockouts unless the full limb motion is written; clean the viewport capture of axes, controllers and frustums. A top-down camera-route sketch works as an image reference ("follow the camera route in @Image 16"). Storyboard grids as input: one image, at most 15 panels official, clean line art, minimal text, declared reading order, excluded style ("do not use the grid's line-art style, text labels or placeholder characters"); independent keyframe images align better than a grid; first and last frame declared separately, same aspect ratio.

Failures: twins or face blending (per-subject binding plus "their movements are not synchronized; clothing, hair and features must all be distinct; no identical clones in the background"); plastic skin (the pores clause, production language); plausibility slips after 30 s (state the world's hard constraints in the tail; errors hide in the back half); a one-take that keeps cutting (2.5 errs by commission: "one continuous shot, no cuts of any kind" plus a camera path that never motivates a cut; or drop to a 10-15 s window); edit stalls (source at most 20 s, one edit target per pass). Economics: about 500-700 credits per 30 s at 720p; draft at 480p, finish at 720p, fix with edit, extend and reshoot rather than regenerate.

### 5.4 Kling 1.x to 2.6

Element limits by version: 1.6 keep it minimal; 2.1 Pro 1080p 30 fps, motion brush, end-frame control; 2.5 Turbo Pro at most 3-4 distinct elements, 50-80 words; 2.6 Pro 5-7 elements, 50-80 words, motion control and element binding. Overstuffing melts faces and hands. Image-to-video on any version: 20-40 words, motion only, "preserve X" cues work well ("preserve silhouette and label text; slow lateral tracking, 85 mm; she turns her head toward camera, wind catches her hair; golden-hour rim light").

Formula: subject with specifics + one clean movement verb phrase + scene (3-5 elements) + camera language + lighting + atmosphere, as flowing prose; tag fragments are disliked.

Negative field, the special rule: the field interprets input as exclusion, so write the thing itself, never "no X". "blurry faces, distorted hands, melted features, extra fingers, watermark, subtitles, logo, text overlay, jitter, shaking camera, deformed." Keep it short; long negative stacks reduce motion and detail.

Element library: 3-4 reference images of the character (front, profile, three-quarter), 1080p or better, even light with no hard shadows (the model can read a shadow as a facial feature), no text or watermark, clean background, centered, well contrasted; enable element binding in image-to-video. Motion brush: up to 6 regions, each with its own path; the text must match the brushed motion (brush a river flowing and write "stagnant pond" and the model tears itself apart). Motion control (2.6 Pro): copy a performance from a reference video; the prompt then describes only the subject. Fast motion morphs: generate in slow motion and speed up in post.

### 5.5 Kling 3.0

Multi-shot up to 6 shots in one generation, native audio with dialogue and lip-sync, up to 15 s, native 4K up to 60 fps, in-prompt character labels. Tiers: 3.0 Turbo (audio included in base cost, best lip-sync per dollar on talking heads, 3-15 s, capped at 1080p), 3.0 Omni (references and editing, 4K in and out, elements with voice binding), base 3.0 for everything else. Reads cinematic intent; prompts are scene directions, not object lists.

Five layers in order: Scene -> Characters -> Action -> Camera -> Audio, as labeled prose. Anchor subjects before any shot with unique labels that survive every shot:
```
[Character A: Exhausted Partner, late 40s, gray-streaked beard, navy peacoat, hollow eyes]
[Character B: Female Investor, early 30s, sharp blazer, calm posture]
Master intent: tense negotiation in a glass-walled office at dusk.
Shot 1 (0-3s). Wide tracking. Character A enters from left, crosses to the table.
Shot 2 (3-6s). Profile close-up on A. He sets down a brown leather folder.
Shot 3 (6-9s). Shot-reverse-shot to B. She does not blink.
Shot 4 (9-12s). Macro insert on her hand tightening around a fountain pen.
Shot 5 (12-15s). Two-shot, low angle, both reflected in the glass wall.
Camera. Slow, deliberate. 35 mm and 85 mm.
Lighting. Cold blue dusk through floor-to-ceiling glass, one amber desk lamp.
Audio. Distant city, footsteps on hardwood, pen clicks. No music.
```
Every shot states framing + subject + motion; two adjacent shots with the same framing and angle merge into one take. Plan 30-60 words per shot.

Dialogue protocol: P1 structured naming (unique labels, never "[Man]"); P2 visual anchor before the line ("Character A pulls a folded note from his pocket and reads aloud: ...") or lip-sync drifts; P3 voice tone in the tag (`[Character A, raspy deep voice]: "We're out of time."`), a generic tag gets a generic voice; P4 linking words between consecutive lines ("Immediately, Character B: ...") or they overlap. Vocabulary obeyed as instructions: profile, three-quarter, macro insert, two-shot, OTS, POV, Dutch; shot-reverse-shot, match cut, smash cut, J-cut, L-cut; tracking, dolly, push-in, pull-out, whip pan, crane, handheld, orbital, Steadicam, "pan right while tilting up"; tempo words "ultra-slow motion" (2-3 s feel), "slow and deliberate" (5-8 s), "moderate" (3-5 s), "quick snap" (1-2 s), or exact ("5-second dolly zoom"). Angle carries power: low = dominance, high = vulnerability, eye level = neutral. In the app, master-shot presets are more stable than hand-written camera prose for standard moves.

API: cfg_scale 0-1, default 0.5; 0.3-0.4 creative freedom, 0.7-1.0 strict adherence for product and explainer work. Per-shot durations sum to at most 15 s. Ratios 16:9, 9:16, 1:1. generate_audio is a boolean and there is no "no music" switch; the model lays music even against a negative, so generate with audio off and add sound in post when you need a clean track. Image-to-video on 3.0: lock first, then move ("Preserve identity, wardrobe and the storefront sign exactly. [Character A: same person from the image] Shot 1 (0-2s) she turns toward camera, exhales. Shot 2 (2-5s) slow push-in; wind catches her hair. Audio ..."). Elements 3.0 on Omni: a 3-8 s video (appearance and voice) or up to 4 multi-angle stills plus an optional 5-30 s voice clip; define once, then tag in prose ("@Grace picks up the folder"); several practitioners report image quality dropping when elements are enabled, so use them only when consistency matters and prefer in-prompt labels for one-offs. Two similar speaking characters need two separate identity blocks or elements, never one shared description.

### 5.6 Veo 3 and 3.1

Native synchronized audio: dialogue, SFX and music generated in sync, lip-sync native. Durations 4, 6 or 8 seconds. Dialogue budget: at most 8 seconds of natural speech per clip; cram more and the delivery speeds up unnaturally; cut the line and test by reading aloud. 3.1 adds image-to-video with a first frame, reference ingredients, stronger motion coherence. Prompt order: subject and action, environment, camera and lens, lighting, style, audio, duration. Sweet spot 50-200 words; shorter gives latitude, longer gives control and more contradiction risk; if the model cherry-picks, compress to 50-100 words or move to JSON.

Dialogue syntax: double quotes plus a lead-in verb (says, whispers, shouts, mutters, asks); the colon form is often more reliable than the comma ("A woman says: "Welcome to the future.""). Voice modifiers before the verb ("He says in a weary voice, ..."; "She whispers nervously, ..."). Audio needs an explicit signal: labels `Audio:`, `SFX:`, `Says:`, or parenthetical events "(a key turning in a lock)". Camera direction ignored: move camera to the front of the prompt ("Wide aerial shot. ..."). Mood words bleeding into object colors: switch to JSON and lock the character in a continuity block.

JSON schema (3.1): `version`, `output` {duration_sec, fps, resolution, aspect_ratio}, `global_style` {look, color, mood, reference}, `continuity` {characters [{id, description}], props [], lighting_constant}, `scenes` [{id, start, end, shot {type, framing, camera}, action, environment, lighting, audio}]. Use JSON for multiple scenes in one generation, strict character continuity, props that must keep color and size, or when prose kept changing colors as mood words were added. Reference ingredients: "the character from reference_1 walks into the location from reference_2 holding the object from reference_3." Image-to-video: "Maintain the subject from the first frame. Slow push-in, 50 mm. She exhales, her eyes shift left, a strand of hair falls. Warm rim light grows. Audio: soft breath, distant traffic. Duration: 6 seconds."

### 5.7 Choosing and the cross-model skeletons

Default picks: multi-shot narrative or fast montage -> Seedance (or Kling 3.0 if dialogue); dialogue, commercial polish, synchronized SFX -> Veo, or Kling 3.0 for multi-character dialogue to 15 s; character consistency across many social clips -> Kling 2.6 Pro (cheaper) or 3.0 with labels; 10-15 s continuous narrative with audio -> Kling 3.0; 15-30 s single-generation arc, heavy reference kits, editing or extending footage, 30-180 s long form -> Seedance 2.5; face-heavy drama -> Seedance 2.5, Kling or Veo. Timecoded beats suit Seedance and Veo; Kling 1.x-2.x prefers flowing prose.

Compression order when a model wants fewer words: keep character continuity, story action, timecodes, lighting, camera, editing grammar, sound; remove philosophy, extra adjectives, director references. Keep the skeleton, lose the perfume. Never stack more than two director references; one dominant reference per scene, translated into camera, light, rhythm and blocking.

Continuity checklist before output: same face, body and hair; same clothes exactly named; location logic; object state progression (fridge -> pot -> fork -> floor); no extra characters; no unwanted text, subtitles or logos; no impossible hand-object action; no vague camera or light; no contradictions; palette in concrete colors; final image stated. Lighting constant repeated verbatim across clips ("cold fridge light as key from frame right, warm window spill as rim from frame left, same contrast ratio in every shot"). On models with unreliable negatives, do not even mention the text or score you do not want; a mention summons it.

## 6. Still-image prompting

### 6.1 Two model families, two syntaxes

The still-image layer sits before the motion layer: identity, composition, aspect ratio, visible text and product state are locked in the image before anything moves, because the video model amplifies every ambiguity in the still. Generate the still at the target aspect ratio (9:16 for a vertical campaign), never crop after animation. Approve a batch of stills through a review gate; only approved frames go to video. The hand-off to the video model is a motion brief (camera move, subject move, background move, duration, framing lock, forbidden changes), not a re-description of the frame.

Model selection:
- Nano Banana (NB2 Lite, fastest and cheapest, 1K only, drafts and batches; NB2, the default workhorse with text, 4K, grounding and consistency; NBP, the top tier for complex multi-layer scenes): natural-language paragraphs, 1-2 paragraphs, order subject + action + location + composition + style. Wins at image grounding (NB2 searches real images before generating: accurate architecture of a named building, correct animal and plant species; not specific people), extreme ratios (1:8, 8:1, 1:4, 4:1 for banners and comic strips), thinking mode (always on for NBP; NB2 has `thinking_level: minimal | high`, raise it for spatial infographics), JSON for 5+ elements, references up to 14 (NB2 Lite 14 high-fidelity objects; NB2 10 objects and 4 character refs; NBP 6 objects, 5 character, 3 style), conversational edits without masks (up to 3 stacked edits keep the source). Do NOT write lens numbers (50 mm, f/2.8, ISO); it ignores them; write "shallow depth of field", "wide-angle distortion". Booster words are inert but crowd out facts. Resolution written exactly as `1K`, `2K`, `4K` (lowercase k is rejected). Workflow: variants at 0.5K or Lite 1K, select, regenerate the winner at 2K or 4K. Known fails: hands and faces still drift (explicit anatomy, references, re-rolls; NBP sometimes holds faces worse than NB2, test both for a portrait series), small text smears at 1K (2K+ or GPT Image), overcooked HDR (ask "natural contrast, no HDR look"), infographic facts can be wrong (verify every number), invisible watermark on all outputs.
- GPT Image 2.5 (Flare, the fast default; Sunburst, the quality base model for precision edits, identity preservation, product and label fidelity): labeled 5-slot template. Wins at identity preservation in edits, small dense text, UI mockups, exact-text brand print, a 5-step `quality` lever (low for previews and volume; medium default; high for small text, portraits, identity-sensitive edits, brand assets; xhigh for fine texture and print; max only when xhigh missed a specific requirement; change one parameter per iteration, quality first, then the prompt), 4K output (3840x2160 or 2160x3840). Sizes: both sides multiples of 16, longest edge 3840, max ratio 3:1 (no 1:8; go to Nano Banana), total pixels 655,360 to 8,294,400; common 1024x1536, 1536x1024, 1024x1024, 2048x1152. `background: transparent` for PNG or WebP (check the real alpha channel, not a painted checkerboard). Booster words actively degrade output here. World-knowledge anchors work ("Bethel, NY, August 1969" summons the whole visual world; "Havana, 1957"; one genre anchor only, never two).

Both: start with a verb (Create, Generate, Design, Transform, Edit); positive framing ("empty street" not "street with no cars", "solo portrait" not "no other people"); be specific (not "shiny" but "brushed steel, matte finish, fingerprint smudges"; not "dark green" but "#0d3d2d"; not "on the right" but "right third, bleeding off the edge"); give the use context (it infers plating, DOF, palette); quote all text exactly with weight, color and position; edit, do not re-roll (80% right means ask for the one change); one change per iteration; index every reference with a role.

### 6.2 The 5-slot template (GPT Image, usable anywhere)

```
Scene: location, time of day, background, environment
Subject: who or what is central, doing what, framed how
Important Details: materials, textures, light source and direction, camera angle, lens feel, mood, composition
Use Case: editorial photograph / product mockup / UI screenshot / poster / infographic / storyboard panel
Constraints: what must NOT change or appear (no extra text, preserve face, no watermark, no people in background)
```
The fifth slot is where mediocre prompts fail silently; without constraints the model drifts. Minimal example: "Scene: small florist storefront at blue hour, wet cobblestones. Subject: woman in a navy apron locking the front door, half turned to camera. Important Details: warm interior glow spilling onto the pavement, 50 mm feel, soft contact shadows, brushed brass handle, hand-painted "Florista" sign. Use Case: editorial photography. Constraints: no extra signage, no people in background, no text other than the sign."

Parameterized version for series (only the variable changes, the structure stays stable so the model sees a consistent format): `Scene: {location, default="marble counter, morning light"} / Subject: {product} on {surface, default="raw linen"} / Important Details: {lighting, default="soft window light from left"}, {lens_feel, default="85 mm feel"}, {key_texture} / Use Case: e-commerce hero / Constraints: clean background, no props except the surface`.

Text in image: literal text in quotes or ALL CAPS; say how many times it appears ("render the tagline exactly once"); font, size, color, position explicit; spell difficult words letter by letter; "no extra text, no duplicate text, no watermarks"; for small text "100 percent readable and physically believable" and quality high at minimum. Text-first hack: have the model write and refine the copy first, then ask for the image with that exact copy.

### 6.3 Keyframes

A keyframe is a single still that must carry one story beat with no motion and often no face. Each panel does four things at once: one readable beat sayable in one sentence; one function tag (Establish, Reveal, Power, Pressure, Detail, Reaction, Shift, Impact, Aftermath, Exit); one emotion routed through metal, fabric, light or anatomy, never named; one implied motion frozen as its physical residue. You cannot prompt "motion" or "emotion" into a still; you prompt the consequence each leaves behind.

Keyframe-to-prompt skeleton, weight at the start:
```
[Framing + lens]            "low bumper-height macro, 24 mm, shot past a blurred roll-cage bar"
[Anchor subject + state]    the ONE object at its threshold: "rear tire sidewall wrinkling at the contact patch"
[Depth clauses, 3 jobs]     foreground frames or obstructs; midground is the act; background is the stake or rival
[Motion-as-still cue]       "sharp subject against horizontally motion-blurred background, foreground cone elongated into a light streak, rim spokes smeared to a disc"
[Light + palette]           one motivated source, its direction, the surface it rims; concrete colors; any color ban repeated
[Texture]                   "fine film grain heaviest in the shadows, halation on the practicals, one anamorphic flare, crushed blacks"
[Face rule]                 "no face" or "driver a featureless silhouette against blown headlights, only knuckle highlights legible"
[Aspect / note]             "9:16; no speed ramp; build velocity into the static frame"
```
Rules: name the differential blur explicitly (models default to over-clean stabilization; equal blur everywhere is a soft photo, differential blur is speed); a frame reads as fast only when at least three of five cues coexist (camera at or below bumper height, ground texture streaking the lower frame, a near foreground object passing the edge, sharp hero against blurred background, a mechanical vibration cue); render sound as physical consequence (heat haze off the headers, chassis squat), never as a named sound; if the panel could be captioned "clean" or "beautiful lighting" it has failed, so name the grain, bloom, crushed black and one specular hit; roughly 70% of the frame unlit, one motivated practical per panel; three depth layers with stated jobs or you have one plane; angle encodes the beat (low = power and launch, eye-level lateral = parity, high = judgment and aftermath); one focal point, zero dead objects (keep an object only if it shows state, advances the beat, or bridges to the next panel; the coffee cup and the dashboard dice go). Density: a silent beat gets one held panel; a bang gets a 6-9 panel burst; no two bursts touch.

### 6.4 Character sheets

Lock one reference frame of the character and point every later prompt at it. Identity lock phrasing: "Using the person from Image 1: keep facial features exactly the same. Change: [expression / pose / clothing / setting]. Maintain: identity, recognizable features." For a series, create the reference in one run, then reuse the image as input and repeat the defining details in every prompt (hair, garments, marks); "same as before" is not enough. Rules for a multi-character story: identity and attire consistent throughout; vary angle, distance and expression; only one of each character per image; generate one image at a time.

Turnaround sheet (3-view): "Scene: clean white background, flat even lighting with no cast shadows, thin #CCCCCC height reference lines at head, shoulder, waist, knee and foot. Subject: {name}, {description}, three views left to right: front, three-quarter turned 75 degrees right, back. Important Details: identical proportions and vertical alignment on the lines, feet on one baseline, arms relaxed for silhouette, color callouts as small circles ({color} {hex}) connected by thin #999999 leader lines to the matching material on the front view, seams and folds consistent across angles. Use Case: animation model sheet, 3D reference. Constraints: no props, neutral standing pose only, exact same character and outfit in all three views, no drop shadows, no gradients." Quality high for the lines and callout text.

Expression sheet: 3x3 grid, thin #DDDDDD dividers, head-and-shoulders, same three-quarter-left head angle, same hair and light in every cell; cells named with body facts ("angry: furrowed brow, clenched jaw, narrowed eyes. Label: ANGRY"); labels in a named font, #555555; constraints: no variation in hair, accessories, skin tone or clothing between cells, head angle fixed, no hand gestures. Outfit variant grid: same pose in every cell, only clothing changes; outfit name below each cell. Reference card: left 40% full body front, upper right three-quarter bust, mid right the signature items at consistent scale and labeled, lower right 4-6 palette swatches with hex codes, bottom strip a one-line stat block; unified style across sections.

### 6.5 Storyboard panels

Sequential images generated one at a time: "Create a {N}-part story. Characters: ... Setting: ... Story arc: ... with emotional highs and lows. Identity: characters and attire consistent throughout. Variation: different angles, distances, expressions. Generate one image at a time. Format: 16:9 per image." Commercial variant ends with "Ending: elegant shot with the product."

One-image grids: number and describe every panel; "show multiple angles" produces an inconsistent grid. Always state the grid dimensions ("3x3 grid, 3 columns 3 rows"), the reading order ("left to right, top to bottom"), the border rule ("thin dark border" or "borderless: no white lines, no black borders, no gaps"), subject consistency with repeated identity markers, and panel variety as a requirement ("no two panels may be identical or near-identical; each must change shot size, angle, action beat or foreground/background relationship"). More panels means less detail per panel: 4 panels allow rich descriptions, 12 need 3-8 words each. Panel count follows scene duration when the sheet feeds a video pipeline: up to 9 s a 3x3, 9-12 s a 3x4, over 12 s a 4x4; every panel 16:9 regardless of grid; two small solid-dark tabs inside each panel carrying the panel number top-left and its duration top-right ("0.5s", "1s", "2s"); durations sum exactly to the scene length (5 s on a 3x3: eight panels at 0.5 s and the last at 1 s; 15 s on a 4x4: fourteen at 1 s and the final two at 0.5 s); trailing panels left black rather than padded. The sheet shows the whole scene in one image; the video is still generated as several clips of about five seconds.

9-cell TVC grid template: "Scene: dark storyboard layout, 3x3 grid of cinematic frames for a {duration}-second commercial; thin dark borders; below each cell the scene number, title and timestamp in small white sans-serif. Important Details: Panel 1 (0:00-{t1}): ...; Panel 9 ({t8}-{duration}): ...; each labeled "Scene N: title" and "start-end". Use Case: pre-production storyboard. Constraints: all 9 panels clearly separated, no merged cells, distinct content per panel, legible timestamps." 1536x1024, quality high (text). Text-heavy grids go to GPT Image at quality high; text-free complex grids to NBP; exploration to NB2 or GPT Image at low.

### 6.6 Removing the generated look

Both families carry a prior toward the idealized image: eyes large, round and symmetric; a V-line jaw and doll proportions; poreless even skin; idealized silhouette; tighter newer clothes; volumized glossy hair; model stance with chin up; influencer casting; saturated tidy background; added earrings, props and signage. A real reference has narrow or hooded eyes, a long or flat midface, pores and redness, an ordinary body, the exact wear of the garment, flat clumped hair with flyaways, an awkward stance, a muted cluttered background, nothing added.

Procedure:
1. Check every axis above against the brief or reference. Every deviation from the prior is stated twice: in the descriptive slot, geometrically and measurably ("narrow eyes with a low single lid crease, iris about 60% covered", not "unusual eyes"), and in Constraints as one labeled imperative per axis (`EYES:`, `SKIN:`, `BODY:`, `HAIR:`, `MEDIUM:`). Constraints is the one legitimate place to name the unwanted version. Nano Banana has no constraints slot: fold the same lines into one closing sentence.
2. Ban the boosters from every positive slot: 4K, 8K, ultra-detailed, hyper-detailed, ultra-realistic, photorealistic render, masterpiece, best quality, sharp focus, crisp, intricate detail, flawless, stunning, perfect.
3. Name a capture pipeline instead of "realistic", stated as a fact of the image, never as a render style ("a real phone photo", not "photorealistic render in phone style"): video frame grab (soft detail, mild compression blocking, slight motion smear); 35 mm color negative lab scan (grain, halation around highlights, soft blacks); phone camera with auto HDR off (smeared shadow noise, faint edge halo, flat tone); mirrorless natural light no flash no retouch (fine sensor noise, natural falloff, unpolished skin).
4. At least three located imperfections, each with a place: "pores across the nose and cheeks, redness at the nostril wings, one mole below the left jaw"; "two flyaways above the crown, ends clumped on the right shoulder"; "creases at the elbow bend, collar askew on the left"; "scuffed baseboard, uneven wall paint"; "one blown highlight on the forehead, slight motion blur on the near hand." An imperfection without a location is ignored or sprayed everywhere.
5. Sharpness and grading: no added sharpening, low micro-contrast; flat or natural grade, muted to moderate saturation, low contrast; ban in Constraints the glow family (HDR, bloom, high clarity, luminous or radiant skin, glossy hair highlights, perfectly even background). On GPT Image stay at quality medium for this work; high sharpens micro-texture until it reads as retouching; go up only when matching a supplied face outranks the unretouched look.
6. List as absent everything the model likes to add that the brief lacks: glasses, earrings, a second person, signage, captions.
7. Self-check: no constraint contradicts a positive slot (asking for grain while banning grain). If the result still misses, treat every mismatch as an under-specified variable: split that description into finer sub-facts, sharpen its constraint line, re-run the same prompt with one change. Re-rolling an unchanged prompt returns the prior.

Hero-shot richness is the inverse discipline (cinematic verbose mode, for the one key visual): add surface wear ("chipped paint on the frame, hairline scratches on the drip tray, oxidation on the steam wand"), micro-textures ("visible pores, individual eyebrow hairs catching backlight, linen weave"), atmospheric particles ("dust motes in the beam, steam wisps"), specular behavior ("caustics inside the carafe, wet sheen on the counter edge"), fabric drape, contact shadows ("where the cup meets the saucer"), environmental reflections ("the APERTO sign glowing on his forearm"), motion cues ("a frozen droplet mid-drip").

Edits, two-column logic: `Change: one concrete thing / Preserve: face, identity, pose, lighting, framing, background, geometry, text, layout / Constraints: no extra objects, no redesign, no drift`. One edit per iteration; repeat the preserve list every iteration ("same style as before" carries context until it drifts). Patterns: garment swap ("change garments only; preserve exact face, body shape, pose, hair, expression, background, camera angle; match lighting and shadows so the outfit looks worn"); object removal ("remove X; do not change anything else"); weather or light swap ("change only environmental conditions; preserve identity, geometry, camera angle, object placement"); translate in place ("translate the text to {lang}; change nothing else", then check for untranslated words); sketch to render ("preserve exact layout, proportions and perspective; realistic materials consistent with the sketch intent; no new elements or text"). Check after every result: text exact and readable; identity, product shape and labels intact; only the requested thing changed; real alpha if transparency was asked.

## 7. Short-form and ad formats

### 7.1 Platform specs

Vertical video canvas: 1080x1920 (9:16), 30 fps, MP4; source of any resolution is center-cropped to fill. Lengths: TikTok optimal 15-60 s, raw native feel, trending sounds, start under 30 s; Reels 15-30 s, polished, rewards saves and shares; Shorts 30-60 s, search applies, titles matter. Hook in the first 1-2 seconds on TikTok; the first frame of a Reel must hook.

Safe zones, the single most-missed constraint: platform UI sits on top of the video. Cross-platform worst case on 1080x1920: keep 220 px clear at the top (tabs and account row), 500 px at the bottom (caption, music and CTA stack), 180 px left and 180 px right (action rail). Result: a 720x1200 centered text band from y=220 to y=1420. Compose every caption and every load-bearing visual inside it; preview against an overlay; re-verify after app updates.

Caption rules: white fill, black stroke (about 8 px), no background pill; bold sans-serif; static captions with no entrance or exit animation (animated captions read as "made by a brand"); each caption at full visibility on the first frame of its window and its window matches its segment exactly; at most 2 lines on screen, 3-5 words per line; highlight key words in a second color; auto-size from about 58 px down in 2 px steps to fit the band, floor about 26 px, never overflow, never clip mid-glyph; captions raise watch time 25-40% because most viewing is silent. Keep captions clear of any baked-in label band (captions around 60% height, label around 80%).

Audio: mute clip audio by default and let one music track carry, with per-clip exceptions (a creator's voice at full, B-roll muted); fade music over the final ~0.8 s (a hard cut to silence reads as broken); for organic posts attach the trending sound in-app rather than baking it in (native audio is rewarded and discoverable); bake music in for paid ads and cross-posts. Voiceover slightly faster than conversation, varied tone, pauses on key points, music ducked under voice. Loudness for delivered files: integrated -16 LUFS, true peak -1.5, LRA 11.

Still sizes: Meta feed 1:1 1080x1080; Meta Stories and Reels 9:16 1080x1920; Google display 1.91:1 1200x628 and 1:1 1200x1200; LinkedIn feed 1.91:1 1200x627 and 1:1 1200x1200; TikTok feed 9:16 1080x1920; X feed 16:9 1200x675, X card 1.91:1 800x418. Export 9:16, 4:5 and 1:1 by regenerating stills per aspect, not by cropping.

Copy limits. Google RSA: headline 30 chars (3-15, any 3 shown together, must make sense in any combination), description 90 chars (2-4), display path 15 chars x 2; mix of 15 headlines: 3-4 keyword, 3-4 benefit, 2-3 social proof, 2-3 CTA, 1-2 differentiator, 1 brand; pin sparingly. Performance Max: 5 headlines at 30, 5 long headlines at 90, 1-5 descriptions at 90, business name 25. Meta: primary text 125 visible of 2,200 (front-load the hook; Stories and Reels overlay under 72 chars), headline 40 recommended of 255, description 30 of 255, display link 40; 1-2 emoji at most. LinkedIn single image: intro 150 of 600, headline 70 of 200, description 100 of 300; carousel intro 255, card headline 45, 2-10 cards; message ad subject 60, body 1,500, button 20; text ad headline 25, description 75. TikTok in-feed: ad text 80 of 100, display name 40. X: tweet 280, card headline 70, description 200, 0-2 hashtags. Spaces count; emoji count 1-2; dynamic insertion needs safe defaults; verify in the platform preview. Adapt across platforms from the most restrictive outward: 30-char search headline -> 40-char Meta headline -> 150-char LinkedIn intro -> 125+ Meta primary text.

Organic caption limits: Instagram 2,200 chars, about 125 visible, 3-5 hashtags (5 max); TikTok 4,000, about 150 visible, 3-5 hashtags (5 max); LinkedIn 3,000, about 210 visible, 3-5 hashtags at the end, 1,200-1,500 chars performs, links in comments; X 280 standard, 1-2 hashtags; YouTube title 100 (about 70 visible), description 5,000, at most 15 hashtags (exceed and all are ignored), the first 3 in the description show above the title; Facebook 40-80 chars ideal, hashtags barely matter; Threads 500, one topic tag; Pinterest title 100, description 500, keywords in sentences over hashtags.

### 7.2 Format taxonomy

The one question that ranks every format: is it a unicorn scaler (punctures cold, net-new audiences and holds as spend scales) or a supporting-cast member (converts people already mid-funnel and will not open new audiences at any spend)? Build a portfolio of both; a supporting format is only "bad" when you expect it to scale cold. Persona-based delivery is why creator-fronted formats top the list: the format does the targeting.

- S tier, invest first: founder content (the reliable first winner at any production level, tell why you built it); partnership ads with creators (the single highest priority; deploy when rolling month-over-month reach falls; have each creator also shoot low-fi statics); the video sales letter (60 s to minutes, hook and problem -> mechanism -> proof -> offer, for anything the buyer must understand before buying; the script is the ad).
- A tier, scales with the right inputs: amateur investigation (a creator tests the category on the viewer's behalf and lands on the product as the finding); the yapper (one creator to camera, hook line first, personal story, product as the turn, 20-60 s; casting is the bottleneck, flat energy kills it); David and Goliath (a real villain: a bloated incumbent, a practice that rips people off); grid statics (multi-SKU, lowest-hanging fruit); authority (a real credentialed expert; closest to regulatory risk); green-screen commentary (creator cut out over reference imagery that changes with each beat); catalog ads run cold, not only retargeting.
- B tier, supporting cast: callouts, before and after (watch claims), progression, tweet and forum-post statics (great first frames), headline ads, us vs them, objection handling (often top 15), founder's letter static (sales periods), conversation (hard to execute), educational infographics, mood boards, ugly handwriting and post-it statics (sales periods), reaction + demo hard cut (3 s reaction with hook caption, hard cut to demo, 9-12 s total; the cut is the mechanic), "no yapping" split-screen tutorial (silent, input left and output right, step captions at the seam).
- C tier, situational: AI animation (hits new pockets briefly, rarely holds), statistics ads (awareness, not direct response), celebrity, AI avatar (fading as brands pick real creators), warehouse, street interview (often better recreated than captured, claims still real), duet or stitch (needs rights), ASMR (needs a real ASMR creator), generic manufactured UGC (fatigued).
- D and E tiers: breaking-news ads, AI billboards, get-ready-with-me, text-only, testimonial statics done badly, listicles, carousels.
- F tier, do not bother: press ads (rights nightmare), fake podcast clips, notes-app and fake-native UI ads (everyone stops, nobody qualifies, the algorithm gets confused).

Faceless motion-style ads (15-45 s): a styled poster still per beat -> subtle image-to-video motion -> narration -> word-timed captions; about 3-6 dollars and 15 minutes per finished video. The still carries the idea (one literal, slightly surreal visual per beat); motion only makes it breathe. Script 3-6 beats, one idea each, calm beats hype, one CTA line. Generate beat 1, approve it, pass it as a reference for every later beat so the set reads as a series. One style per campaign (screen-print collage, flat vector explainer, papercraft diorama, pop-art comic, claymation, or the brand-token styles: monoline editorial, Swiss typographic, dark wireglow, duotone screenprint, each driven by FIELD, INK, one scarce ACCENT and TYPE FEEL). Motion prompt: "Subtle living motion of the existing elements only. <one literal motion tied to the concept>. <secondary ambient drift, gentle push-in>. Every element visible now is the only thing that ever appears; the composition stays exactly as it is. Everything stays <flat printed collage / cut paper / clay>. No camera whip, no scene change, no morphing, no added text." Gotchas: video models add photoreal "maker hands" on pressing motions, and "no hands" makes it worse (never mention hands; give the motion to the objects); QC the final 2 seconds of every clip, that is where intruders and style drift appear; one dominant motion per beat; text-to-speech and the transcriber disagree on sound-alikes ("laws" vs "loss"), read the transcript before burning captions; put the brand in the poster itself so it survives sound-off autoplay.

Founder and organic vlog structures: hero's journey (problem -> backstory -> attempt -> failure -> epiphany -> breakthrough -> cliffhanger; one strong story a week beats daily noise), math (a fixed-budget breakdown; unexpectedly cheap outperforms luxury), shiny object (something novel you have access to: the factory, the machine, the trade show; never money as the object), niche guide (the world through your professional lens, filmed in the world). Three-capture shooting: every moment close, medium and wide (0.5x); 2-3 second clips only; if the subject moves hold the phone still, if nothing moves add a slow push or slide; do the activity first, then grab three angles of 10-12 things in five minutes; favorite the single best hook shot; at least 5 shots of yourself. Edit at 0.5-1 s per shot: record the talk track first, lay clips under it, reorder, trim.

### 7.3 Writing ad variations

Grounding first. Most AI ad generation fails on inputs, not output: keep a corpus of 10-20 screenshots of the brand's winning ads from the last 90 days, 50-100 customer reviews, and the top comments on existing ads (objections become FAQ ads, unprompted praise surfaces angles you did not write). Every concept cites its source; no invented claims, stats or testimonials; verbatim customer language beats paraphrase; separate language evidence ("the export takes forever", which supports a pain angle) from claim evidence (a measured reduction across a defined pilot, which alone supports a number). If the corpus is empty, stop and ask; never generate ungrounded concepts as a fallback.

Angles before lines: 3-5 distinct reasons to click, each a different motivation: pain point, outcome, social proof, curiosity, comparison, urgency, identity, contrarian. Then vary within each angle by word choice, specificity (numbers vs claims), tone (direct, question, command), structure (short punch vs full benefit). Ten hooks across ten segment x motivation cells beat thirty rewordings of one. Headlines: specific ("cut reporting time 75%") over vague; benefit over feature; active voice; numbers; no "best, leading, top", no all caps, nothing the landing page cannot deliver. Descriptions complement rather than repeat: proof points, objection handling ("no card required"), a reinforced CTA, genuine urgency only.

Awareness stage sets the treatment: unaware needs to recognize a lived moment (feeling: "Leave Friday with the forecast finished"; conversation: two short lines, "Is this the final sheet?" / "There are three final sheets."); problem-aware needs a credible path out (pain + outcome under ten words; time-anchored friction: "Month-end still starts with copying CSVs"); solution-aware needs the concrete difference (state change: "Bring the three approval threads into one queue"; a documented statistic with population and period); product-aware needs an objection resolved with proof. For text-led statics, the headline is the visual: one dominant sentence occupying roughly 50-70% of the frame, intentional line breaks, logo secondary, preview at actual mobile feed size, never solve legibility by shrinking a required qualifier.

No AI tells, across the whole batch: no "it's not X, it's Y" contrast reveals; no negation lists ("no setup, no fees, no contracts", keep the one that matters); no trailing pile-ons after the claim; no self-answered questions or colon reveals ("The result? 3x faster."); no stock phrases ("say goodbye to", "X, reimagined", "unlock the power of", "take it to the next level", "here's the thing"); no em dashes; no generic "POV:", "stop scrolling", "what nobody tells you"; no "get started today" or "join thousands of happy customers" (name the action and what they get); no broetry (one short line per line for a whole post), no engagement bait ("agree?", "let that sink in"), no emoji bullets, no manufactured vulnerability. Vary sentence shapes across a batch; ten variations opening the same way read as generated even when each is fine alone.

Variation process from data: pull performance by the metric that matters (CTR, conversion, ROAS); identify winning themes, structures, word patterns and lengths; identify what falls flat; generate new creative that doubles down on winners with fresh phrasing, extends winning angles, tests 1-2 new angles, avoids loser patterns; log the round (top performers, patterns, counts, angles tested and retired). Change one variable per test cycle; allow 1,000+ impressions before judging; generate in waves (core angles 3-5 x 5 variations, then extensions of the top 2, then wild cards); filter for limits, near-duplicates and policy; check that headline and description combinations make sense together. Fidelity ladder: hunches ship as low-fi statics, text-on-screen video or voice-over-B-roll within a day or two; only angles that showed a funnel signal earn creator shoots and staged demos. Production tiers: T1 iteration (hours, new hook or crop on an existing asset), T2 remix (days, new creative from existing footage or generation), T3 production (weeks, only with own-account proof or a prior low-fi signal). Roadmap to real capacity; a 20-concept slate against 8 of capacity produces 20 compromised ads.

### 7.4 What the platforms measure, and how to read it

The delivery funnel isolates one component per metric; read the whole funnel before declaring a winner:
- Stop: thumbstop or 3-second view rate. Weak means the visual action (and caption) failed. Fix: a new visual opening, everything else unchanged.
- Stay: hold rate from 3 s to 15 s or 50% view. Weak means the on-ramp (seconds 3-15) failed, not the hook. A good on-ramp logically extends the hook's premise; a bad one pivots to a product pitch. Every hook test is also an on-ramp test: swapping a hook onto an existing body breaks the bridge, so rewrite the on-ramp per hook.
- Click: CTR. Weak means the promise, proof or CTA mid-ad.
- Convert: post-click conversion. Weak means the page does not continue the ad; fix the page or the claim.
A great thumbstop is not a great ad: clickbait visuals show as high thumbstop with collapsed hold and conversion. One component per iteration.

Organic signals: watch-time percentage and completion rate (did hook and content deliver), saves (worth revisiting), shares (worth spreading), comments (sparked talk), follows (wanted more). Pivot rules: 5+ videos under 1% completion, change hooks; high views and low follows, check the CTA and audience fit; high saves and low shares, valuable but not social; many negative comments, adjust tone or lean in. Comments outrank reactions outrank clicks; dwell time signals quality; first-hour engagement matters; external links in the body kill reach on most feeds; on X write for the share and the follow, name the topic in the first 10 words, wait about 60 minutes between original posts, be present for the first 30 minutes. Judge concepts, not ads: three executions of one concept failing says the concept is wrong; one failing says the execution was. Every learning lands somewhere (icebox, re-rank, kill) or the review was a meeting.

## 8. Pre-flight checklist before a frame is generated

Run top to bottom. A "no" anywhere sends you back to that section, not forward to the render button.

1. Idea: one stranger's sentence of 25 words or fewer; a named tension; a named emotion at Tier 2 or 3; the brand swapped for a competitor breaks it; the level (brand, campaign, execution) matches the brief.
2. Hook: three components written (visual action, spoken line, caption) that do not repeat each other; the top two of five scored hooks kept; the on-ramp for seconds 3-15 rewritten to extend the hook's premise.
3. Lines: a gerund under every line; names covered and still distinguishable; nobody tells anybody what both know; the last word of each speech is the cue; the scene's value charge differs between first and last line; enter late, leave early.
4. Shots: the five anchors named; every shot card has all 14 fields; every shot has three details and does one of three jobs; one dominant camera move with a stated reason; geography in one sentence; a pause before the biggest cut; the final image written.
5. Prompts: identity block repeated verbatim in every clip; every reference has a written role and exclusions; no banned words; negatives written in the form this model honors; duration split to the model's reliable window; the end state of every clip or stage stated.
6. Stills: target aspect ratio from the first generation; deviations from the beautifying prior stated twice; a capture pipeline and three located imperfections for anything that must pass as a photograph; boosters absent.
7. Platform: everything load-bearing inside the 720x1200 safe band; captions static, white on black stroke, at most two lines; copy inside the character limits of every placement; music baked only where a native sound cannot be attached; sound-off version still makes sense.
8. Review of the output: the final two seconds of every clip checked for intruders and drift; hands, faces and text inspected at full size; object states consistent across clips; the whole timeline watched once with sound off and once with eyes closed.
