# The principles

Rules that came from a demanding client's corrections across two films, each with the failure or the reason behind it. They are written as rules because they were learned as corrections; a new client may rule differently on the few that are taste, and the file should then change the same day.

## What a film may show

1. **Only what the product really does.** Every result on screen is a real result; every line the product says or writes is what it really says or writes. A film shows nothing the product cannot do that day. When the client writes a line the product cannot yet make true, the film keeps the line and the capability goes on the product's backlog as top priority the same day: "it needs to be true." A demonstration in an ad must show real performance; a disclaimer does not cure a misleading one (the law, see `legal-and-truth.md`). A launch film that showed two wrong answers from the device became the story of the launch.
2. **Never defend against a weakness the product does not have.** The client cut "nothing to install, nothing to learn" from a script: "why say it in the video when it's supposed to be the marketing video." Saying it plants the doubt.
3. **Show the strong things and the whole job.** Not only the simple things everyone shows (a booking, a bill paid); the trip booked end to end with the itinerary as a little book, the price watched and bought inside the limit, the extra step nobody expects (the number added to a Do Not Call list, the rental car reserved). "It does the whole entire thing to really help them."
4. **Generated people are characters, never customers,** and the end card says they are generated and that what the product does is real. No real person's face or voice; check every generated face for a resemblance.
5. **Never name a model, a vendor, or how the product is built** in anything public.
6. **Against rivals: show, never compare,** and compare against all of them in your own research, not only the newest. A film that shows the product doing the thing stays true; a film that says "they can't" is wrong the week they can. The named comparison belongs in a post that can be edited. The first pass of one film measured the product against one rival only and called a capability unique that two others had; re-read every rival before writing and again the week of release.
7. **Nothing branded is generated by a model.** The wordmark, the words on screen, the card, any logo: made in code from the brand's own files. Models garble text and draw logos from memory.

## The hook

8. **Every film opens on its hook.** "A video without a really, really good hook in the beginning is worthless." Frame 1 is the hook and the poster: no card, no logo, no room first, no disclosure line (that goes on the end card). The first 1.5 to 3 seconds are designed before anything else. A film built first and given a hook later is a film built twice.
9. **The hook is a person saying something to you,** felt and not acted: "You gotta hear this. This is insane." "Emotionally, but not too much, because then it sounds fake."
10. **The poster frame is chosen by hand:** a face with a specific feeling, cropped tight, at most three elements, zero to four words, no logo. On X and LinkedIn frame 1 is the poster.

## The script

11. **The client edits the script himself, in a plain file,** before anything is generated from it. Not an online document he may lose access to when an account changes; a file in his downloads folder and in the editor, with the picture, the spoken line and the words on screen per scene and a notes space. He will edit it by hand; read every change.
12. **Write it the way he writes it** (learned by comparing his edit with the draft, line by line):
    - People talk the way people talk: "it literally does everything for me", "he's like, what the heck", "yeah sure", "should be ready after two". Lines are said, not written.
    - The big plain claim first, then one proof.
    - Word of mouth carries the story: "my son told me about it", "I told him about it; two days later he called me".
    - The person on camera tells what happened between people; the footage shows what the product did. Leave the surprise for the video; do not explain the feature in the line.
    - Outcomes, not images: "and it's done", not "and I'm in the garden".
    - Made-up proper names make it real: the pharmacy has a name.
    - The benefit is a feeling in plain words: "I don't stress anymore".
    - The product's name is the last word of the last line.
    - The product on a call is short, human, and asks: "Is that fine?" rather than "You're booked."
    - No clever rhythm: no "and it did", no "two weeks later", no sentence fragments for effect.
13. **The structure follows the teller.** In a story told to camera: she tells it, then we see it, and what we see is always her or a person she has just named. A cutaway to a stranger the teller never named breaks the film ("you see a guy trimming something, and it's not about the old lady").
14. **A second piece is its own piece.** A fast run-through of what the product is, a vertical cut, a type-only version: made and posted separately, never generated into the main film.

## Voices and sound

15. **The product's own voice is the client's pick by ear and the film borrows it;** the film never invents its own. If the product has a second voice for a kind of call, the film uses that too.
16. **A proper name is pronounced the way the client says it.** Speech models read a written name their own way; find the spelling that comes out right by making several and listening, and use that spelling in every text handed to a speech model, while the written brand stays as written.
17. **Every voice is approved by the client before it is used,** from numbered candidates on a page he can open, each list labelled by the person in the film.
18. **Every voice sounds fully human:** imperfect, with breath and hesitation, a regional colour. "The more perfect it is the less human it sounds." Nothing hand-made, nothing the video model invents for a person on camera.
19. **One voice at a time.** Two at once sounds like a bug. The mix is built from the edit list, every line at its real length with a gap, everything else ducked under it.
20. **Someone on camera who is not speaking keeps his mouth shut:** he listens, smiles, nods on the strong lines. An audio-driven model animates every face in the frame to the voice unless masked; the listener is generated separately and composited.
21. **If the product speaks to the public on a call, it says its name and on whose behalf;** whether it announces that it is an AI is the client's and the law's decision, never the film's.

## The words on screen

22. **Every word on screen is a speech bubble with a tag** saying who speaks: "You" for the person, the brand wordmark for the product, the other party's role for the other end of a call. It pops up beside the speaker on the empty side of the frame, sits on glass that lets the picture through, points at the speaker with a squared corner, and never crosses into another scene.

## How the work is shown to him

23. **One thing at a time.** One question, one decision, then the next.
24. **One piece at a time, raw.** For a new film each shot is generated alone, shown raw (no words, no music, nothing on top), judged, and the lesson carried to the next shot, "so we don't generate the entire video for nothing." He can only teach taste piece by piece.
25. **Raw footage is kept clean.** The takes never carry letters or toppings; everything on top is a separate layer that costs nothing to change.
26. **Everything is made in small pieces:** short clips when generated, and the film rendered one shot at a time and joined, "because if we need to change something, we don't need to change the entire thing, just that piece."
27. **He reads the chat, not files.** Files he must open go to his downloads folder with plain names; previews under the chat's size limit go in the chat; the master stays on disk. A question he cannot answer now becomes a numbered item on a list he can come back to.
28. **Movie grade:** several angles and close-ups of anyone who talks, cut like a film. A single angle of a talking person is a slideshow.

## The seat

29. **The video maker produces the films.** Anything the film needs changed in the product goes on the product's backlog with the client's words, what gets built, why it matters now and the priority; no product debates in the film conversation.
30. **Everything learned is written down the same day,** in one place, organized so the whole system can be copied elsewhere and work.
31. **The footage lives only where it was made.** Generated footage is too large for a code repository; it stays on one computer unless copied. Back it up before the computer is replaced; the client who moves to a new laptop otherwise finds nothing.
