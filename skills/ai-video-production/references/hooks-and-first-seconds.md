# Hooks and the first seconds

What the numbers say, the hook types with their mechanism and failure mode, the poster frame, and what the best launch films did shot by shot. Measured in September 2026 on thirteen launch films of assistants and consumer AI, and from fifty-one sources on hooks and thumbnails. "Hook rate", "thumb-stop rate" and "3-second view rate" are one measurement: plays that reach 3 seconds, divided by impressions.

## 1. The numbers

- Up to 47 percent of a video campaign's value is delivered in the first 3 seconds and up to 74 percent in the first 10; 65 percent of people who watch the first 3 seconds watch at least 10 (Meta with Nielsen, reported 2016). People who watched under 3 seconds of a video ad still produced a 47 percent lift in ad recall (Nielsen for Facebook, 2015). Impressions under 2 seconds drive 38 percent of brand recall (Nielsen, 2017).
- Cold feed ads on Meta: 3-second plays over impressions run 18 to 28 percent in feed (median about 23) and 24 to 36 on Reels (median about 30), aggregated benchmarks 2025 to mid-2026. In plain words: 7 to 8 of every 10 people shown the ad are gone before second 3. Judge a hook against 35 percent and above on Meta and 70 percent and above "viewed versus swiped away" on YouTube Shorts (5,400 Shorts with 3.3 billion views: under 60 percent rarely performed; the best sat at 70 to 90). "Treat your intro like a thumbnail."
- TikTok's own guidance: the proposition in the first 3 seconds, the hook within 6, on-screen text at 5 to 10 words a second. Google's ABCDs (11,000 ads, 2021): "Drop viewers in from the start", "engaging pacing and tight framing", brand in the first 5 seconds; 30 percent lift in short-term sales likelihood for ads that follow them. X: the key visual at the 3-second mark, 15-second ads, captions.
- Sound: 69 percent watch with sound off in public; 80 percent are more likely to finish a captioned video. On TikTok 88 percent say sound is essential and 73 percent would stop for audio. LinkedIn autoplays muted. So: the hook lands silent (words on screen, a picture) and rewards sound on.
- Long-form: the first 10 seconds decide; a creator predicted a 40 percent drop at second 12 of a video he clicked off at 8, and the analytics matched. "No wasted words. Short, concise, and then tension."
- Base rates for launch films: median 28,000 views; 8 percent pass a million; founders appear in about 80 percent of the top tenth; a text post with four real outcomes beat most films. 86 percent of launch videos do not reach 50,000 views.

## 2. The mechanism

Curiosity is a felt gap in knowledge (Loewenstein, 1994): it peaks with a SMALL gap, so the viewer must already half know the answer. An open loop pulls (Zeigarnik, 1927): an interrupted task intrudes until it is closed; the pull is real even where the memory effect is not. The hook stops the scroll and adds no value on its own; the story and the offer do (Brunson). Spend as much time on the opening line as on all the rest; use callouts for the cocktail-party effect; show the hook as on-screen text, not only speech (Hormozi). Layer: a spoken hook and on-screen text stating the same idea in the first frame.

## 3. Fourteen hook types, each with mechanism, example and failure

1. **Pattern interrupt.** The feed has a texture; the hook breaks it; the brain orients to change before it decides anything. A romance film revealed at second 7 to be playing on a phone; black and white photography in a colour feed. Fails when the interrupt is unrelated to the product: people stop, then leave, and the retention curve punishes the video after second 3.
2. **Curiosity gap.** Enough to know a question exists, not enough to answer it. "Can you tell which one of these ads is AI?" Fails when the gap is too big (no foothold) or the answer is withheld past the format's patience (on Reels the payoff is inside the clip, by second 6).
3. **Open loop.** A story started and not closed. "I tied up an FBI agent. I have $100,000 in this bag. Here's the knife, good luck." Fails when the loop closes with less than promised; expectations set at the click must be met or average view duration collapses.
4. **Cold open mid-action.** No setup, no moment to swipe; the viewer is inside a scene with stakes. A date already going wrong with a glass panel between the two; a woman already on hold on speakerphone. Fails when the scene needs context, or when the first frame is a wide (a tiny figure on a trail needed a huge title at 3.5 s to hold).
5. **A bold claim.** A declarative the viewer wants to test. A founder on a sofa making the claim before three real demos. Fails when not demonstrated by second 10, or when it is the category's claim and not the product's ("your personal AI agent" is now every assistant's sentence).
6. **A question.** Processed as if asked in person; the viewer answers in their head and is in. Fails when the answer is an obvious yes or no, or when it asks the viewer to do work.
7. **"You" address.** The viewer recognises themselves in the first words: a forgotten subscription demanding its monthly payment. Fails when too broad ("busy people") or in the product's jargon.
8. **Contrast or reversal.** The expected thing set up and inverted in one cut: an assistant that says no. Fails when the reversal is a stunt with no truth behind it.
9. **Emotion first.** A face doing a recognisable feeling in a tight frame; mirror response before comprehension. A man's face against firework bokeh. Fails with generated faces that are a little wrong, and with generic emotion (a smile) instead of specific (relief at a phone that did not need answering).
10. **The outcome before the cause.** The result is a fact on screen; "how" is the gap. Fails when the outcome is small or unreadable in a glance, or the how turns out ordinary.
11. **A sound hook.** A sound with a reflex: a ring, hold music, "your call is important to us". Fails on sound-off feeds unless the sound is also shown (a waveform, a caption, a hold counter).
12. **Text on screen.** The only hook that survives muted autoplay, read faster than a picture is understood. Fails past about 7 words, or in a typeface that reads as template.
13. **The triple.** The same idea on three channels (seen, said, written) in the same half second, so it survives every viewing condition. Fails when the three disagree.
14. **Proof first.** A receipt, a transcript, a real result before any claim: a founder's text post with four named outcomes did two million views without a film. Fails when the proof is mocked up: a launch film with two wrong answers from the device became the story.

## 4. What worked and what did not, across the field

Worked: a face with a specific feeling; a sentence of proof; a small true panic the viewer has had (a name you cannot remember); a sound everyone hates (hold music). Did not: a logo or a stage first; a cartoon standing in for the person; emotion the AI takes from the human (a father using AI to write his daughter's letter was pulled after backlash); and a hook that was not true: the truest hook in the field (an assistant that tells you the name of the person approaching) was pulled and litigated because the feature did not exist. A hook is only as good as the truth check behind it.

Measured on the top films (first 3 seconds): a man's face in firework bokeh, the wide at 3.8 s, no logo, no product (3.2M views for a small company); a dim restaurant wide with a glowing panel between two people and a very large name at 1.5 s (13.2M, controversy did the distribution); a locked-off wide of a trail and a huge serif name at 3.5 s (26.6M; the premise carried it); kinetic type with no people for 82 s (7.8M on the incumbent's own distribution); a founder on stage (11.1M, then the product failed reviews); five silent title cards (3.8M). The two most viewed both put a huge wordmark over picture within 2 to 4 seconds and showed no demo by second 10: a story film can break the demo rule if the first image is striking and the name arrives early.

## 5. The poster frame

- On X a free account's poster is frame 1; LinkedIn autoplays muted so frame 1 is what is seen; TikTok picks a frame unless chosen and crops the profile grid to about 3:4 with a caption band over the bottom 270 px; Reels picks from the first second or two and crops the grid to the middle 55 to 60 percent; 90 percent of YouTube's best performers have custom thumbnails, and its test runs three variants on watch time, not clicks, over up to two weeks.
- An aggregate of 3,200 thumbnails and 127 tests (2026, a vendor): high contrast 6.5 percent CTR against 4.7; a face 6.4 against 4.8; a tight face crop (chin to brow at least 40 percent of the frame) 7.6 against 4.3 for full body; under four words of text 30 percent higher than text-heavy. Test order: contrast, then the face's expression, then text density.
- Rules: frame 1 is the poster, chosen by hand, reading in a quarter second; a face with a specific emotion beats an object, tight beats wide; at most three elements; maximal contrast; zero to four words; nothing in the top and bottom 15 percent of a vertical frame and nothing in the bottom fifth of a landscape one; no logo in frame 1 (the name arrives large between seconds 2 and 5, over picture). The cheap test first: five strangers, one second each, "what is this about?"

## 6. Structure, length, pace, sound (measured on the top films)

- Story-led hero films ran 83 to 104 seconds (one at 157); product-only films 47 to 72. Advisers say 75 ideal, 90 ceiling; X ads 15 s; vertical cutdowns 15 to 30 s, one idea each, re-cut and not cropped.
- Average shot 2 to 3 seconds, median near 1.5, inserts as short as 8 frames; every top narrative film has one long hold at the emotional peak (19, 17.5 and 31 seconds). Fast, fast, fast, then stop. Six bursts of 3 to 6 cuts at exactly 8-frame spacing marked chapter changes in the best one.
- Hold a question open from the first seconds to the end: something visibly unresolved that then changes. A returning object is a callback, not an open question.
- Teach the unit twice, fast (two asks inside about 6 s), then escalate one axis at a time: effort saved, then stakes, then trust. Put the thing nobody else can film in the last third and give it the long hold. End on character, then the card: wordmark, one line, address, 4 to 6 quiet seconds.
- The product is the narrator: every story beat a readable message of 7 words or fewer; no voiceover; works with the sound off. Films built on speech burn captions in from the first half second.
- Unbroken audio through the film; the end card often silent. The product's own sounds (send, receive, ring, pickup) are the score's percussion.
- Post: a 16:9 master, 9:16 cutdowns, 4:5 or 1:1 for feeds; copy under 100 characters; Monday to Wednesday morning Pacific; the poster frame chosen by hand; the founder answering every reply for 48 hours. "The first 30 to 60 minutes decide everything."

## 7. Coverage for "a person asks, it gets done" (per vignette)

1. Establishing wide, 1 to 2 s: the person small in a real place. 2. Medium or over-the-shoulder as the thumb moves. 3. Insert on the thumb, 0.3 to 0.8 s. 4. The sent words, readable in one glance. 5. Macro of the reply: two words filling the frame. 6. Reaction close-up: an eye, half a smile. 7. The result in the world: the payoff; the cut from 5 to 7 is the magic. 8. Optional: the phone face down while life continues. Transitions: a match cut from one send to the next (same hand, same frame size); the next scene's sound under the current reply; the send sound carried over the result. Pick one way of showing the message (the phone held centre with the real interface; a bubble beside the actor; no live action; a handheld take of the real phone) and hold it for the whole film.

## 8. Twelve hook shapes that scored well for an assistant that answers your phone (as a worked example of scoring)

Each was scored 1 to 10 on stop power in a silent feed, relevance (does it promise what the product sells), truth (literally what it does today), and cost with generated footage. The four that tied at the top were different kinds: a lit phone ringing "Unknown number" and a hand that hovers and does not answer, "You didn't answer. It did." at 2.4 s (stop 8, relevance 10, truth 9, cost 8); hold music and a running counter over a phone face down at dinner, "It is on hold. She isn't." (8, 9, 9, 9); two amounts on a bill, the second circled, and the message "I didn't pay it. Want me to call them?" (7, 9, 9, 10); a call transcript typing itself, "I'm calling on behalf of ..." (6, 10, 10, 10). The lesson of the scoring: the strongest stop power (a face in a meeting, a phone buzzing at the frame's edge) was the most expensive to make true with generated faces (two speaking people, lip sync), so the film opened on the cheapest true hook with the highest relevance and kept the expensive one for the day a real recording exists.
